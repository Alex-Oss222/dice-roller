# Shared economic reference

Source workbook: [Westeros_283_AC_Economic_Model.xlsx](Westeros_283_AC_Economic_Model.xlsx)

Use the workbook to ground the material scale of Westeros when economics actually affects an outcome. It is a modeling reference, not automatic canon and not a turn-by-turn accounting system.

## What it should inform

Consult it when the story materially involves prices, wages, hiring, household expenses, land or holdings, rents, taxes, debt, loans, trade, transport, provisioning, construction, harvests, military supply, ransom, dowry, valuable property, shortages, or the economic scale of a decision.

Do not consult it merely because someone eats a meal, rides a horse, enters a market, or owns ordinary possessions unless cost or supply becomes consequential.

## Realistic use

1. Start with the nearest relevant value, category, or relative scale in the workbook.
2. Check units and what the value actually represents before using it.
3. Adjust for place. Local production, distance from supply, road or sea access, tolls, storage, perishability, market size, security, and merchant risk all matter.
4. Adjust for circumstances. Harvest quality, season, war, siege, levies, requisition, disrupted roads, unusual demand, disease, political disorder, and shortages can matter more than the passage of years.
5. Check availability before affordability. Having enough coin does not create grain, horses, ships, craftsmen, wagons, or labor where they are unavailable.
6. Account for status and institutions. Great houses command stores, rents, retainers, credit, obligations, and bulk purchasing. Lesser households have less reach. Smallfolk may rely partly on barter, dues in kind, informal credit, or household production. Armies can consume stores, purchase supplies, requisition them, or forage, with different political consequences.
7. Use ranges or approximate scale when the evidence does not support an exact figure. Do not manufacture precise prices from a broad estimate.
8. Keep GM knowledge separate from character knowledge.
9. Record an economic fact in a story's WORLD.md or CHARACTER.md only when it becomes a lasting fact that may matter later.

## Story 1: 283 AC

The workbook is the direct-period baseline.

Even in 283 AC, do not copy a table value blindly. Robert's Rebellion can alter supply, labor, transport, credit, risk, requisition, and local prices. A besieged town, marching army, intact rural estate, major port, and isolated village should not behave as one market.

## Story 2: 298 AC

The workbook is a fifteen-year-old structural and price-scale anchor, not an exact 298 AC price list.

Do not apply a blanket compounded inflation rate. Preserve the workbook's broad relative economic relationships unless there is a reason for change, then adjust from actual conditions in 298 AC: region, local production, long-season conditions, trade access, political stability, demand, transport, scarcity, and any story-specific divergence.

If a price or economic fact has been established inside Story 2, that story fact takes precedence over a generic 283 AC baseline for later events unless circumstances change again.

## Divergence

Each story may develop its own economy through war, deaths, destroyed holdings, marriages, trade decisions, rebuilding, debts, harvests, political changes, or other consequences. Those story-specific facts belong in that story's WORLD.md and override the shared baseline where they conflict.
