# Story 3 · Jon Stark · Third day of the first moon, 126 AC

## Established backstory, 124–126 AC

The following is the player's accepted alternate-history premise, replacing the previous starting setup. These events have already happened; they are not pending attempts or turns to replay. This is campaign canon, not a claim that the assassination and transfer occur in the published books.

In 124 AC, sixteen-year-old Cregan Stark was Lord of Winterfell in name, but his uncle Bennard Stark still controlled the castle, treasury, and northern administration. Bennard had served as regent since the death of Cregan's father, Lord Rickon, in 121 AC. When Cregan reached his majority and demanded his authority, Bennard refused to surrender it.

Later that year, Bennard arranged his nephew's murder with the assistance of his eldest son, Benjen. A bribed stablehand weakened Cregan's saddle before a hunt in the Wolfswood. The young lord was thrown from his horse and suffered a severe head injury. Cregan's consciousness died, although his body remained alive. At that same moment, Jon Stark, formerly Jon Snow, was drawn through the weirwoods from 487 AC into Cregan's body by the Old Gods, who had resurrected Jon centuries earlier.

Jon arrived with the memories of his own 204 years and inherited Cregan's memories in full, including his childhood, family, friendships, education, and knowledge of Winterfell. Cregan's original consciousness was gone permanently. Jon's former body died in 487 AC, and none of his possessions or animal companions accompanied him. The transfer was a singular supernatural event, not an ability he could repeat.

Jon spent the remainder of 124 AC recovering and adjusting to his circumstances. By 125 AC, he was entirely comfortable in Cregan's body, including its strength, movement, swordsmanship, and physical limitations. His inherited memories allowed him to continue Cregan's existing relationships without confusion. More importantly, Jon retained the judgment of a man who had commanded armies, ruled an independent North for sixty-two years, and spent more than a century studying and traveling after his abdication.

During 125 AC, Jon quietly secured the loyalty of Winterfell's household, established relationships with the Cerwyns, Glovers, and Norreys, and investigated Bennard's administration. He uncovered the payments connected to the attempted assassination and gathered evidence of Bennard's misuse of his regency. Rather than begin a northern civil war, Jon isolated his uncle politically and secured enough armed support to remove him.

In late 125 AC, after gathering his evidence and support, Jon took control of Winterfell. His supporters secured the gates, relieved Bennard's captains, seized the treasury and regency records, and arrested the conspirators. Bennard and Benjen were convicted of treason and attempted murder and sentenced to lifelong imprisonment under separate guard at Winterfell. Jon spared their lives rather than execute members of his own family. Bennard's other sons, Brandon and Elric, were not implicated in the assassination. They were removed from Winterfell and placed as political wards with Houses Glover and Manderly respectively, under formal oaths recognizing Cregan's authority and renouncing any challenge to his succession. Bennard's wife, Margaret Karstark, was permitted to return to Karhold.

Jon also confiscated 20,000 gold dragons from Bennard. At the opening, this additional coin remained available in Winterfell's treasury under Jon's control.

With his authority secured, Jon reorganized Winterfell's administration. He dismissed corrupt officials, audited the treasury and noble obligations, established regular reports on harvests and winter provisions, and reorganized the castle's garrison under captains personally loyal to him. He ordered repairs to neglected roads, bridges, and defensive positions, strengthened patrols along important routes, and worked with House Manderly to improve the movement of grain and commercial goods through White Harbor. Northern lords were required to provide accurate accounts of their available men, supplies, and outstanding obligations. These were practical reforms supported by existing northern institutions, not an attempt to remake the kingdom overnight.

By the third day of the first moon, 126 AC, Jon had fully established himself as Lord of Winterfell and Warden of the North. He was eighteen in body, with more than two centuries of experience. He no longer struggled with Cregan's identity or physical limitations, and his household accepted his greater maturity as the result of his injury and the struggle against Bennard. Arra alone knew the truth of the transfer.

Arra Norrey had been Cregan's childhood friend and lover, and she had loved him romantically. Jon possessed Cregan's memories of their upbringing and shared history, so their familiarity was genuine to him, even though his own experiences and personality were different. Before the opening, he told her the truth of the transfer because he trusted her as Cregan's friend. Arra knew that he was Jon and loved him as a friend. They had not married or become formally betrothed; their current relationship was friendship.

At the opening of 126 AC, Jon governed personally from Winterfell, leaving daily household matters to his steward while retaining control over justice, appointments, military readiness, and relations with his bannermen. He knew from his extensive historical studies that a Targaryen succession war was approaching, but had made no commitment to either faction. His immediate priorities remained northern stability, sufficient winter reserves, reliable roads and defenses, and keeping the authority he had recovered firmly in the hands of House Stark.

## Turn 1 | Tenth day of the first moon, 126 AC | Evening

Elapsed: Seven days, from the morning of the third day to the evening of the tenth.

### The returns

Jon took the covering sheets first and set them in the order the steward had used since the autumn: the twelve estates by the road that reached them, Winter Town, the castle, then the tribute roll. The clerk read the totals aloud and Jon followed on the fair copy with the previous account open under his hand, checking the carried figure at the head of each sheet against the closing figure of the one before.

The treasury count was the one made at the seizure, when his men had carried the regency chests into the vault and counted them under three sets of eyes. It had been corrected twice since for the quiet moons. The clerk read it as it stood on the third day of the first moon.

“Coin in the vault, one hundred eighty-nine thousand six hundred dragons. That includes the twenty thousand taken from your uncle's own chests, which are kept in their own strongbox and marked. Owed to Winterfell and not paid: nine thousand four hundred in tribute from the second instalment of last year, the Dreadfort and the Last Hearth owing the largest shares, and about twelve hundred in tenants' cash rent across the twelve estates, with eighteen hundred quarters of grain rent still in the tenants' barns. Grain in the castle granaries and the estate stores, sixty-six thousand two hundred quarters at the harvest count, less what the kitchens and the garrison have drawn since.”

“And the hole.”

The clerk turned two sheets. “What the regency drew between the year your father died and the seizure, and could not account for: thirty-eight thousand, near enough. That is my reckoning from the gaps in his rolls, my lord, not a count. The twenty thousand we recovered is separate.”

Jon wrote the figures in the margin himself, each once, and drew a line under them. He asked what was coming in. The clerk had it ready. The first instalment of tribute fell in the fourth moon, six parts in ten of the year's roll, something near a hundred and nine thousand dragons if every lord paid in full, and the Crown's share, twenty-four thousand five hundred and some, went south in the same moon. Until then the estates brought in a little under five thousand a moon and the household and the works spent a little under two. Grain went out of the stores faster than it came in until harvest, as it did every year.

He asked whether the twelve estates were all reporting on the same day. The steward said eight were and four were not, and named the four. Jon told him which two he wanted brought into line by the second moon and which two could wait for the roads.

The White Harbor letter was from Lord Desmond. It said Elric had arrived and was lodged with the household, that he was quiet and rode well, and that the lord's own sons had taken him hawking. It gave the count of grain lighters sent up the White Knife before the river closed at the bend above the Hornwood landing, eleven, and the quarters they carried. It asked, in the last paragraph, whether the Crown's customs men at the port were to see the Stark grain pass as before or whether the new arrangements changed that. Jon read the paragraph twice and put the letter under the ledger without answering it.

### Arra

He sent the clerk to find her. She came in from the yard still in her riding coat with mud to the knee and took a piece of bread off the tray before she sat.

“You had the household papers,” she said. “Was it bad?”

“Better than I thought. Worse than he said it was.”

“He said it was fine.”

“He said it was fine.”

She ate the bread. He waited until the clerk had gone out for more ink and the door had shut behind him, and then he said what he had sent for her to say. She had kept his secret. She had kept her own. She had been the first person at Winterfell to know who he was and she had helped him take the castle anyway. Whatever she wanted to do now, inside the walls or outside them, she should say it and she would have it, and his full support behind it, and nothing owed for it.

She did not answer that directly. She asked whether the Norrey's tribute had come in full, and he said it had, the clan had paid in the eleventh moon in sheep and silver, and she said good, because her father would ask her, and she would not have wanted to lie to him about that as well.

“I don't care what the kitchens say about me,” she said. “I never did.”

The clerk came back with the ink. She stopped talking until he had set it down and gone out again.

“I want to go up to my father's,” she said then. “Not for good. The track past the Flint's stone won't carry a horse until the thaw, so it will be the third moon before I can. I'll stay until then. I don't want rooms over the gate, or a title, or to sit at your left at the high table so that Cerwyn's wife can count the days. I want to see my father and my brothers and sleep in a hall where nobody looks at me when you come in.”

He said she would have an escort of her own choosing and a letter to the Norrey in his hand.

“He'll ask me what you mean to do about a wife,” she said. “Every lord between here and the Wall is asking that. Don't answer it now. I don't want it said to me in this room with the papers out. Think on it, and tell me before I go, so that I know what to say to him.”

She took another piece of bread, said the grey mare in the second stall had a loose off-fore shoe and someone should see to it before the farrier went to Winter Town, and left.

### The week

On the fourth day the captains gave their muster in the small hall. Each read his roll: men present, men on patrol, men sick, men at the works, and the arms and mail issued against each name. The captain of the south gate had sixty-two men on his roll and fifty-nine present, two on the kingsroad patrol toward Castle Cerwyn and one with a broken wrist. The captain of the east gate had four men short of his roll and could not say where two of them were, and was told to know by morning. Jon had the rolls copied fair and signed each sheet. The garrison's bread and ale were drawn against the signed count from then on, not the roll.

On the fifth day he held the gate court. A carter from Winter Town had lost a wheel on the kingsroad south of the castle where the road gang had cut the old surface away and not yet laid the new. He wanted the price of the wheel and the day's carriage. The foreman said the gang had set a pole and a hurdle at the cut and the carter had driven around them. Two men who had been on the road said there had been a pole and no hurdle. Jon gave the carter the wheel at the wright's price out of the works account and nothing for the day, and ordered a hurdle and a man at every cut from then on, charged to the works.

On the sixth day he rode out to the cut with the foreman and the steward. The old road had been laid on the mud and had sunk. The gang had dug it out to the gravel, a foot and a half down for forty paces, and were laying field stone on edge in the trench and ramming it. Jon paced the trench, had the foreman show him where the next cut would be, asked how many loads of stone it took to the ten paces, and asked where the stone came from. The foreman said the old field wall at the Cerwyn boundary. Jon told the steward to find out whose wall it was before any more of it was carted, and to pay for it if it was not his.

The clerk brought the patrol returns in each evening. The road east toward the Hornwood was quiet. A party of six men without a lord's badge had been seen twice on the Barrowton road and not stopped; the second time the sergeant had sent two men after them and lost them in the Rills. Jon had that written down with the dates and the sergeant's name and left it there for now.

On the eighth day the steward came with the Winter Town tallies and said the brewer's widow had asked whether the lord meant to hold the fair in the second moon as the old lord had, because she needed to know how much to malt. Jon said he had not decided. He had the fair's old takings found and put on his table.

On the ninth day he answered Lord Desmond. He thanked him for Elric and the hawking, confirmed the lighter count against the steward's own tally from the Hornwood landing, which was eleven, and wrote that the Crown's customs men were to see Stark grain pass as before and that no new arrangement changed the Crown's due. He read it over, struck out one sentence about the White Knife ice that told Manderly nothing he did not know, sealed it, and gave it to the porter.

On the evening of the tenth he had the week's drawings against the vault read to him. The works had taken three hundred and six dragons, the household two hundred and ninety, the farrier eleven. Nothing had come in but the mill tolls. The clerk asked whether the fair takings should be set with the household papers or kept apart. Jon said apart. The grey mare had been shod. The steward said the Flint's stone track would not clear before the third moon at the soonest, which Jon already knew, and asked whether the letter to the Norrey should be drafted now or nearer the time.

## Turn 2 | Thirteenth day of the third moon, 126 AC | Morning

Elapsed: Sixty-three days, from the evening of the tenth day of the first moon to the morning of the thirteenth day of the third moon.

### The roll

Jon had the roll read in the solar over three mornings, with the steward, the stores officer, the watch officer and the maester at the table and a master carpenter from Winter Town standing because there was no fifth chair. He took it in the order it was written: the castle first, the town second, his own estates third, the other lords last. At each line he asked the same three things, whether the thing was wanted, whether it could be begun before the thaw, and who would answer for it, and the clerk wrote the answers in a new ledger with the roll's four place-names at the heads of its sections. The works account was the first charge on the fund, twelve dragons for ledgers, sheets, ink, six chests and a seal, and it lived in the back half of the clerk's own room behind a new lock.

The stores officer took two days over the existing granaries before he would say whether new ones were wanted. He went through the three castle granaries with a rod and a tally clerk, measured the floors and the height of the heaps, counted sacks where the grain was sacked, and turned a sack out on a cloth in each bay to look at the bottom of it. In the old north granary the roof had been patched on the outside and not the inside, and the heap under the patch had gone grey for a yard down. He had the bad grain shovelled out and weighed before it went to the pigs. He wrote down what each granary held, what it could hold if it were sound, and how much had stood in the yard under hides at harvest because there was no floor for it. The figures came to the solar on the third day: fourteen thousand two hundred quarters of sound covered room, a little over two thousand quarters of room lost to damp, and perhaps three thousand quarters that had been kept in the yard or sent down to Winter Town for want of space. He said two new granaries, not ten, and the north roof stripped and relaid.

Jon ordered the two. Timber could be felled now. The woodland lease men cut and sledged oak and pine while the ground was hard, and the carpenter marked the frames in the castle woodyard. The foundations would wait for the thaw. The same went for the three wells. The watch officer and a well-digger walked the precinct and fixed sites in the grain yard, the stable court and behind the smithy; the digger put a bar into the ground at each and got two feet of frost before he struck anything soft, and said he would not dig through it for any lord's money because the sides came in when it thawed. The master weights went to the castle smith, who said he could make the standard chest and perhaps six sets before the shearing and wanted a wool merchant to prove the scales against. Sixty dragons went to him for iron.

The paving was the one work that frozen ground helped. The roll wanted stone stocked while wagons could cross the fields, and the field wall at the Cerwyn boundary was not going to supply it. The steward found the old pit on the kingsroad south holding, two miles below Winter Town, where the road gang's gravel already came from; the hillside above it was loose rubble under a foot of turf. Twelve hired wagons and the castle's own six went down and back twice a day when the days allowed it, and the gate tally counted the loads in. By the tenth of the third moon there were a thousand and forty loads stacked along the approach outside the south gate, and the hire and the quarrymen had cost a hundred and thirty-eight dragons. The carpenter said that was a third of what the approach and the market square would want. Jon told him to keep hauling until the ground softened and then stop, because he was not going to pay to drag wagons out of a field.

Winter Town went by the reeve. He walked the streets with the carpenter, a well-digger and a boy with a knotted line, measured the market square, the main street and the lanes, and brought back figures smaller than the roll had guessed: the square a hundred and thirty feet by ninety, the lanes nearer two miles than two and a half. Of the ten wells he found four old ones that wanted lining and a new windlass and would serve, and marked six sites for new ones. He had the privies marked and would not have one of them within sixty paces of a well. The fire casks were ordered from the cooper, thirty dragons down. The hall, the weighhouse, the warehouses, the stable and the winter hall would be tendered when the carpenter could stand on the ground. The hot water was the question nobody could answer yet. The castle's springs ran out under the east wall into the ditch, and whether a wooden pipe could carry that outflow four hundred yards to a bath in the town without freezing in the ground was something the carpenter would not say until he had dug a trench and seen.

### Arra's reading of it

He gave Arra the roll on a morning when she had the tack room to herself and was taking a girth apart. She read it with the straps across her knee and said nothing until the part about the other lords.

“You've written the same letter to my father that you've written to Bolton.”

“The same questions.”

“He won't answer questions on a sheet. He'll answer a man at his table.” She turned the page back. “And ravens. There's no bird that knows the way to the Norrey's hall or the Wull's, you know that. These go up by hand when the track's open, or they don't go.”

She went through the clans' part of it the way she would have gone through a horse she was being sold. Wells were no use in the hills; there was a spring behind every hall and the villages sat on streams. A grain store at the mouth of each valley was use, if it was the clan's store with Stark grain in it and not Stark's store with a Stark keeper, because nobody up there would carry his harvest into a building with another man's lock on it. Road poles on the track past the Flint's stone were worth more than anything on the list, and the clans would cut and set them themselves for the price of the axes and a day's meat. And he was not to offer her father an advance against profit, or a share of anything.

“He'll hear debt. Tell him what you want and what you'll give for it. If you want to give him something for nothing, say that, in those words, and he'll take it or he won't.”

He asked what she thought of the rest.

“I think you'll have a castle full of clerks and a town full of baths and nobody to sit in them until the lords see the first granary stand up. Build the two granaries and the wells and go and look at Cerwyn's fords. The rest will keep.” She put the girth back together. “I don't want any of it. I told you.”

### Ravens and riders

The maester had the rookery book out that afternoon. Of the thirty-five houses that owed Winterfell tribute direct, twenty-six had a maester and birds trained to Winterfell; to those the letter went by raven, the same letter under the same seal, with a second sheet for Cerwyn, Manderly, Dustin, Glover and Reed asking that the houses beneath them be given the same offer. There was no bird for Greywater Watch, and a rider went down the kingsroad to Moat Cailin to wait for a crannogman, with the letter and ten dragons for his keep. The six clans' letters were written and put aside to go up with Arra's escort. Skagos would wait for a ship from White Harbor after the ice. The ravens went out over three days from the sixteenth, and the maester marked each in the book with the day and the bird.

Jon wrote four more letters himself under the common one: to Lord Desmond for factors, buyers, weaving masters and two warehouse floors at the port; to Dustin about hides and bark; to Glover and Locke, asking plainly whether there was navigable water on their land before anyone spoke of a wharf; and to Karstark and Ryswell about stream falls and fullers.

### The ride south

He left on the second day of the second moon with eight men and was at Castle Cerwyn by dark.

Lord Cerwyn took the matter of the field wall before Jon could raise it. It was his wall, it marked the boundary, it was a bad wall that his grandfather should never have built out of road stone, and the gang could take what was left of it; he would put up a proper one in the spring. Jon said the stone would be paid for. Cerwyn said it would not. The steward's account for the second moon shows eight dragons paid to Cerwyn's steward for sixty loads of boundary stone.

The rest took two days with Cerwyn's maester and his reeve at the table. Cerwyn wanted the two bridges and knew where: the ford on his own road below the castle, where carts went in to the axle in the spring, and the Acorn Water crossing on the kingsroad where the gang had already been cutting. He had no need of four wells and said two; the other two villages had water. He would take the two granaries. Of his four parts in ten he would pay nothing in coin and everything in timber from his own woods and his tenants' labor at the dead of the year, valued at the book's rates, which Jon accepted. His maester would survey the two fords when the water was down and send a sworn sheet, and nothing would be signed before that. Cerwyn asked which week the Winter Town fair would fall in so his own market day did not clash with it. He also said, over the second night's supper and in front of his wife, that a lord with no heir was a lord with a question hanging over every bargain he made, and that every man Jon would write to in the next two moons would be asking it. Jon said he knew what they would be asking.

Torrhen's Square was three days west. Master Tallhart met him in the yard and walked him down to the river before he had taken his cloak off, to show him the ford on the Barrowton road where the spring water came down off the hills and carts could not cross for three weeks in some years. He did not want a market hall. He had a market and it was the size it was. He wanted the bridge and the four wells, and if those came out of the same purse as the hall he would rather have the bridge. Jon said they did not, and that a bridge was a public work on the same terms as Cerwyn's. Tallhart would pay his four parts in labor and gravel from his own pit, and asked whether Stark's carpenter or his own would set the piles. Jon said his own, under Stark's carpenter's eye, and Tallhart said that was the right answer.

Barrowton was three days more. Lord Dustin was courteous and had no use for most of what Jon had brought him. Barrowton had tanners, six of them, and had had them for a hundred years, and bark came up from the Rills in the autumn at a price. He would not take Stark coin into a tannery on a profit share. If there was more leather to be made he would make it himself, and he said so twice. The two wells he would take, and in place of the hall he asked for twelve miles of gravel on the road east from Barrowton toward the kingsroad, where his cattle went down to the drove road and his carts went down to nothing. Jon put that in writing as an application against the road miles, Stark six parts and Dustin four in labor and teams. When the unbadged riders on the Barrowton road came up, Dustin's steward said they would have been his lord's horse-wardens, who had been out after a wolf pack that had taken foals from the river pastures in the eleventh moon, and who wore no badge because they were herdsmen and not sworn men. Jon had the sergeant's dates read out and the steward said the dates would fit. It was a report, and Jon wrote it down as one.

He was back at Winterfell on the twentieth, having met three lords and shaken hands on three bridges, eight wells, two granaries and twelve miles of road, none of it signed.

### Answers

The maester had the answers in a box. Bolton's had come first, in a clerk's hand under the Dreadfort seal: the Lord of the Dreadfort thanked Lord Stark for his letter, would consider which villages lacked water, had no present need of a market hall, and would send the arrears of the second instalment with the first instalment of the year. It said nothing else. Umber's had come a week later in what was plainly the lord's own hand. Four granaries, yes, before anything else; the Last Hearth lost grain to damp every winter and he could show the heaps. Coin he had none to spare and would give timber and men. And the two thousand four hundred dragons Winterfell said he owed were grain he had sent up to the Wall on Bennard's order in 124, to be set against his tribute and never set, and he would have Lord Stark's clerk look again before he paid a stag of it. Karstark's was correct and cool: the two granaries were wanted; Karhold had fleece and no fullers and would try a spinning house with its own women before it spoke of a mill. It said nothing of Margaret Karstark, who had been home since the twelfth moon.

Glover's steward came in person on the twenty-sixth with a letter and news of Brandon, who was well, kept to his lessons, and had been taken to the Bay of Ice to see the fishing. On the wharf Glover was plain. There was no landing worth a thousand dragons within a day's cart of Deepwood Motte and he would not pretend one. He asked instead for a timber bridge on the Wolfswood road to Winterfell and gravel on the worst six miles of it, which served Stark's carts as much as his own.

Lord Desmond's letter came on the twenty-fourth and was the longest. He would send two factors up at the thaw to see the wool and the town. Two warehouse floors at the port could be had at a rent. He had written to a Braavosi house that dealt in cloth to ask after weaving masters and would promise nothing. He did not want an advance for a hall and a weighhouse; White Harbor had both, and he would build whatever else it needed at his own charge. He asked instead that Stark's sealed measures be made in White Harbor, where the smiths made scales for the trade and where the merchants who would have to trust them could watch them made. The last paragraph said that Elric had been sick a week with a winter cough and was mended, and that Lady Manderly asked to be remembered to Lord Stark.

Lord Hornwood rode in on the third day of the third moon and stayed two nights. He had read the part about the tar kilns to his charcoal burners, who said it could be done with the old pine on the north side of the Hornwood and a clay-lined pit, and that they had seen it done in their fathers' time. He did not want Stark's two hundred dragons. He would build two kilns at his own cost and burn two trials in the summer, and what he wanted from Jon was a buyer, because tar in barrels was no use to him in the Hornwood. Jon said Manderly's factors would see the trial barrel. The hall could wait until there was something to sell in it. They spent the second evening over the Hornwood's own account of men and grain, the first of the lords' accounts Jon had read beside its author.

Winter Town held its fair in the last week of the second moon as it had in Lord Rickon's time, on the customary day and with the customary stall penny and no other charge, because the maester had found no charter and no record of any toll beyond the penny, and Jon would not add one on the strength of nothing. The brewer's widow had malted for it. ### The fifth day

He found her in the stable court on the morning of the fifth, before he had written a line. She set the foot down and took up the next.

“You know what's coming. I told you. She finished the foot and straightened.

“Tell him that.”

“I'll tell him you're not marrying this year and you're not marrying me, and he'll say he never thought a Stark would marry a Norrey, and he'll be glad of the salt.” She went round to the garron's other side. “What he'll want to know is whether I'm sent home. He said she would have whatever she asked.

“Nobody put me anywhere. I came down on my own feet in 125 and I'm going up on them.” She lifted the off hind and looked at the frog. “Keep the sorry. You'll want it for something you did.”

### The letter to the Norrey

He wrote it that afternoon in his own hand and took the rest of the day over it. ### The west tower

He went up the same morning, with the steward and nobody else. The guard on the stair was one of the south gate men he had put there in the twelfth moon, and he opened the door without being told to and shut it behind them.

Bennard had two rooms, the outer one with a table and the inner one with the bed, and a brazier, and a window with a bar across it that looked over the godswood wall. He had lost weight since the trial and his beard had gone grey on one side.

“You asked three times. What is it?”

“To see my son.”

“No.”

They dug three feet into it, laid the stone lining up from the bottom on a timber curb, and built the kerb and windlass above. The water was tested by letting it stand in a jar for a day and then drinking it, which the digger did himself. The well in the stable court went down fifteen feet and came in brown, and the digger put his nose to the bucket and said it was the dung heap on the other side of the wall and had been for a hundred years, and that no lining would keep it out. Jon had it filled and the site moved outside the court to the lane behind the kennels, which cost the twenty dragons already dug and would cost the whole price again. The third well, behind the smithy, was begun in the last week of the fourth moon.

The two granaries went up on their pads. ## Turn 4 | Fifteenth day of the seventh moon, 126 AC | Evening

Elapsed: Seventy-five days, from the evening of the thirtieth day of the fourth moon to the evening of the fifteenth day of the seventh moon.

### The Norrey

They came down out of the hills on the twenty-eighth day of the fifth moon, thirty-one of them on garrons and on foot, with Arra riding beside her father and the Wull on the far side of her on a horse too small for him. The watch officer had them counted at the Hunter's Gate because that was the standing order for any armed party, and the Norrey watched him do it and said nothing until the count was done, and then said he had been counted into Winterfell by three Starks and this was the first one who had got the number right.

Jon had them at the high table that night, the Norrey on his right and the Wull on his left and the Liddle, whose knee had let him come, at the end where he could stretch it. The mead was the castle's own, from the hives behind the godswood wall, and the Norrey drank the first cup and put it down and waited. Jon stood and said what he had written, in the hall with the clansmen at the lower tables and the household behind them: that as long as he lived Arra Norrey had mead at his table and shelter at his hearth, asked or unasked, and so had the Norrey and any of his blood, and that he said it before the Wull so that it would be known in the hills from more than one mouth. The Norrey said that would do. The Wull said it would do if the mead held out, and drank.

The next morning the Norrey walked the works. He went through both new granaries with the stores officer, got down on his back on the floor of the first to look at the piers from underneath, and asked why the floor was two feet off the ground and not three; the stores officer said two was what the roll paid for and three was what the rats would have preferred, and the Norrey laughed and said he would have his at three. He looked at the timber in the woodyard and chose it himself, pine for the frame and oak for the sills, and said it was to be carried to the Flint's stone before the first snow and no further, because beyond the stone his own people would carry it and no Winterfell cart would get up the ledge. The store would stand at the mouth of his valley where the track came out of the trees, and he would take six hundred quarters of oats and barley into it the first autumn, and the key was his.

The Wull said he would have one too. The Norrey said the Wull had not been asked. Jon said the Wull could be asked, and the Wull said then he was asked, and that was written down with the rest, timber to the foot of his own track and the same key.

They hunted on the first day of the sixth moon, in the Wolfswood below the Hunter's Gate, for boar, because the kennel master had a sounder marked in the oak scrub by the Acorn Water and the Norrey had said he would not go home without seeing the Stark on a horse. There were twenty of them and the hounds. The sounder broke when the hounds found it and the old boar went back through the scrub on his own, as they do, and the hounds held him in a hollow under a fallen oak where a horse could not go. The Norrey offered Jon the spear. Jon got down, took the boar spear from the huntsman, checked the crossbar with his thumb, and went in on foot with the Wull's son on his left and the huntsman on his right with a second spear. The boar came out of the hollow at the man who moved, which was the Wull's son, and Jon stepped across and put the spear into the chest behind the shoulder as it turned and let the crossbar take it, and the weight of the beast drove the butt into the ground and held. The huntsman's spear went in behind his. The boar took a long time to die, as they do. The Wull's son had a cut along the forearm from a tusk where he had been slow with his own spear, and Arra washed it with wine from the saddle flask and bound it and told him he had been slow.

The Norrey said nothing about the kill until they were riding back, and then said that the boy who had ridden the hills with his daughter would have gone in from the front and been gutted, and that whatever the fall had done to the Stark it had put sense into him, and that he was sorry for it in one way and not in another. Jon said he had been sorry for it in both ways and had got over the one.

They went up on the fourth of the sixth moon with forty axes, sixty pounds of bar iron and the promise of the timber. Arra went with them. She had said very little to Jon in the week beyond the business of the store and the Wull's son's arm, and on the last morning she said she would come down for the harvest if the Flint's track held and not before, and that the bath keeper he was looking for was the brewer's widow, who had asked her about it in the yard.

### Stone, wood and wool

The approach was paved by the twenty-eighth day of the sixth moon, from the south gate to the kingsroad, a quarter of a mile and twenty feet wide, rammed and crowned with its gutters led to the ditch. The stone had run out in the fifth moon as the paviour had said it would, and the steward had found more in the hills on the Cerwyn boundary, where the grey rock came out of the turf in slabs that could be broken on the ground. Lord Cerwyn sold it at the ordinary price and his tenants hauled it at the ordinary rate. The same rock, split and dressed, made the setts for the market square, which the paviour priced at five hundred and forty dragons against the roll's four hundred and ninety-six, because dressed stone was dressed by men who wanted paying for it. Jon took the price and the square was begun in the seventh moon. The first cart over the finished approach was the brewer's widow's, with malt, and the carter said it was like driving on a table.

The brewer's widow took the bath. She leased it for three dragons a year and the firewood from the lord's cut at the Wolfswood lease price, and asked that the tubs be oak and not pine because pine wept, and the steward bought them so. The house was framed by the fifteenth and the boiler was a copper from a White Harbor smith at a price that made the steward write a letter about it.

In Winter Town the market hall, the weighhouse, both warehouses and the winter hall stood at their sills and rose through the sixth moon. The reeve walked them every third day with a knotted line and wrote the heights down, which the carpenter resented and Jon required. The infirmary and school houses went up faster because they were plain. The privies were in use and the lanes were cleaner for it; the night-soil contract went to a man from the mill-and-bridge villages with a cart and two sons.

The shearing on the northern sheepwalks gave the lord's share of the wool in the sixth moon, and three thousand stone of it was counted into the castle wool store against the roll's first-season trial, and valued in the works book at what it would have fetched raw, so that the cloth could be judged against it. A hundred and ten spinners were engaged across the sheepwalk and meadow villages at the ordinary wage, paid in coin at the month's end against the yarn they brought in by weight. Of the nine weavers in the town, four would weave Stark yarn at Stark's wage. The other five said they had never woven for a wage in their lives and would not start; they would buy yarn from the lord at a price and sell the cloth where they liked. Jon took the five on those terms and set the yarn price at what the spinners cost plus a tenth, because five weavers at their own risk were worth more than five sulking at his. A fuller in the town was engaged to full the first cloth by foot in troughs until there was a mill, and said he could do two hundred yards a week and would not promise more.

The thirteen village wells went down through the fifth and sixth moons under two digger crews. Seven were finished by the fifteenth of the seventh moon. The well at the mill-and-bridge village went to thirty feet without water and was abandoned at the roll's price for nothing. A well at the east meadow village, lined by the second crew in a hurry, slumped in the first heavy rain and was dug out and relined by the first crew, with the second crew's pay for it stopped.

The castle smith delivered the standard chest and six sets of measures in the sixth moon. A Winter Town wool merchant, a careful man who had been cheated by scales for forty years and knew every way of doing it, proved the scales against the chest with the steward watching, and sealed the six sets. Jon had one issued to Winter Town's weighhouse when it had a roof, and five held for the first five lords who asked. Lord Desmond's letter about White Harbor's smiths was answered with an offer: the other thirty sets could be made there against the Winterfell standard if a Winterfell man sat with the smith and the merchants who watched them made put their own marks on the receipt. Lord Desmond had not yet replied.

### Letters

On the sixth day of the fifth moon Jon wrote to the Dreadfort and to Barrowton in his own hand. Each letter said that if the lord had another proposal Winterfell would hear it, and that if he disputed the note he should say so plainly and send his copy. Neither had answered by the fifteenth of the seventh moon, which for Barrowton was late and for the Dreadfort was ordinary.

Lord Umber answered the grain settlement in the fifth moon in his own hand. He took the eight hundred, and he would pay the sixteen hundred when his four granaries stood and not before, because Winterfell had kept his grain money three years and could wait a year for his. Mormont's tribute came by sea to White Harbor in the sixth moon and up the kingsroad from there, short of nothing but time, with a letter asking for four fish-drying stations on Bear Island and a granary at the harbor, and offering labor and timber for both. Both coastal Flints paid in the same moon. Locke's balance came with his maester's sworn survey of a tidal landing on the Bite below Oldcastle, nine feet of water at the ebb, a gravel bank and a cart road, and his application for the wharf and ten fish stations. Ryswell paid nothing more and wrote nothing.

The crannogman came to Moat Cailin at the end of the fifth moon and took the letter, and the rider came home. No answer had followed him.

### The Acorn Water

He rode down to the crossing on the fifth day of the seventh moon when the river was at its lowest, with the carpenter, the steward and the foreman of the road gang, and Lord Cerwyn met him there from the other side with his maester.

The Acorn Water at summer water was forty paces of gravel shallows with a channel on the north side under a bank of soft black earth that the river cut back every spring. The ford was where the gang had been cutting the road down to the gravel. The marks of the spring flood were on the alders eight feet above the water. The carpenter walked the north bank with a pole and found no bottom under the soft earth for twelve feet, and said what Cerwyn's maester had said: no thirty-foot timber span would reach, and a timber bridge with a pier in the channel would have its pier under eight feet of ice-laden flood every spring and would last as long as the first bad one.

Jon asked the foreman how many carts crossed in a year. The foreman said he had counted none, but the gang had pulled eleven out of the ford since the first moon and had stopped counting after that. Jon asked Cerwyn how many of his tenants' carts went to Winter Town by the ford. Cerwyn said all of them.

He decided it standing on the gravel. The kingsroad was the one road in the North that every house used, and the Acorn Water was where it broke. The roll allowed one stone bridge of a single arch at an ordinary site; this site was not ordinary, and the bridge would be a stone arch anyway, because nothing else would stand there, and the excess would come out of the roll's allowance for difficult sites and, if that ran out, from the reserve. A mason who had built in stone would come up from White Harbor to look at the footings and price it, and nothing would be cut until he had. Cerwyn would give his four parts in ten in sand, gravel, lime-burning and labor at the book's rates, as he had offered for timber, and keep his court over the south bank. Cerwyn said he had never thought to see a stone bridge on the Acorn Water in his life and would believe it when he drove over it. The carpenter said that was the right attitude.

They ate at the ford from Cerwyn's saddlebags, bread and cold mutton and a skin of his own ale, and Cerwyn's maester drew the channel and the banks on a board for the mason, with the flood marks on the alders measured from the water.

On the evening of the fifteenth of the seventh moon the running count stood at three hundred and fourteen thousand eight hundred and sixty dragons, the late tribute from Mormont, the Flints and Locke in. The roll had taken three thousand one hundred and one dragons in all and stood committed for about two thousand more under tenders and work in hand, with the arch unpriced. The steward said the timber for the Norrey and the Wull was cut and stacked at the Flint's stone track head, and asked whether it was to go up before or after the harvest carts were wanted. Jon said after.

## Turn 5 | Thirtieth day of the tenth moon, 126 AC | Evening

Elapsed: A hundred and five days, from the evening of the fifteenth day of the seventh moon to the evening of the thirtieth day of the tenth moon.

### Harvest

The carts began coming up the approach in the third week of the eighth moon, from the home farms first and then from the meadow villages, and for the first time in the memory of anyone at the granary door there was a floor for everything that came.

The stores officer had the doors of the two new granaries open from dawn and a clerk at each with a tally board. Each cart was driven under the eaves, the sacks were counted off the tail and the count cut into the carter's stick and the clerk's, and the sacks went up the ramp to the raised floor and were stacked in bays by kind, oats on the left and barley on the right and rye at the far end, with a hand's width between the stacks and the wall. The old granaries filled as they always had. The new ones took what in other years had stood in the yard under hides or gone down to Winter Town on borrowed floors. On the twentieth day of the tenth moon the stores officer gave Jon his count: seventy-four thousand one hundred quarters in the castle granaries and the estate stores together, against sixty-six thousand two hundred at the same count the year before, a good harvest and a dry one; nothing at the castle under hides; nothing sent to the town for want of room. He had also counted what the old north granary held under its new roof, and it was dry.

Jon asked him what the two new buildings had saved. The stores officer said he could not say what had been saved, only what had not been lost, and that he had never been able to count what rotted in the yard because nobody had ever weighed it going in. Jon said that from this harvest he would weigh it going in, and the clerk wrote it as a standing order.

### The ford

Cerwyn's carpenter drove the piles for the castle-road bridge in the last week of the eighth moon, when the river under the castle was at its lowest. Jon rode down for it with his own carpenter and stayed two days. The piles were oak, pointed and shod with iron from Cerwyn's own smithy, and driven with a ram on a tripod by six men on the ropes, two blows to the count and the count called by the carpenter's boy. The two bents went in on the gravel at ten feet from each bank, the caps were pinned, the stringers laid across and the deck planks spiked down, and the approaches ramped with the spoil from the road cut. It was a plain bridge of the kind every ford in the North could have had a hundred years ago and did not. The first thing over it was a Cerwyn hay cart, and the second was Jon.

There was a quarrel to settle before he left. Cerwyn's tenants had cut the stringers from a wood on the boundary where the Winterfell woodland lease men claimed the fall of timber, and the lease men had come to the carpenter with their lease. Jon and Cerwyn heard both at the bridgehead. The lease ran to timber felled for sale; the bridge timber was not for sale. Jon ruled the stringers Cerwyn's contribution as agreed, and gave the lease men the brushwood and tops from the felling, which they wanted for charcoal and would have taken anyway, and both sides went away saying they had been robbed, which Cerwyn said was how he knew the judgment was fair.

Master Tallhart's bridge at the Barrowton-road ford went in the same way in the ninth and tenth moons, with his own carpenter and Winterfell's watching, and was finished on the twenty-fifth of the tenth moon. Jon did not go. Tallhart's letter said the piles had gone in sweetly and the deck was level and he had driven the first cart over himself, and that two of his four wells were dug, and that his tenants had asked when the other three bridges were coming, which he had told them was not a question for him.

### The mason

Master Wyl came up from White Harbor in the eighth moon. He was a short man with a limp who had built the quay wall at White Harbor under Lord Desmond's father and the porch of the new sept under Lord Desmond, and he looked at the Acorn Water for three days before he would say a word about money. He had the carpenter's soundings and Cerwyn's maester's drawing and he did his own, with a boy holding a rod in the channel while he sighted along a level.

His price was five thousand nine hundred dragons, against the roll's four thousand three hundred and sixty for an ordinary site. The difference was the north bank. The arch itself was ordinary: thirty-two feet of clear span in the grey hill stone, which was sound and near, turned on a timber centering and backed with rubble. The south abutment would sit on the gravel. The north abutment would not. It needed a coffer of driven piles and clay, the soft black earth dug out inside it down to the gravel, which was twelve feet, and the abutment built up from there on a raft of oak and a bed of lime concrete. Without that, he said, the bridge would stand until the first spring the river moved its bank, and then it would stand with one foot in the water and the other in the air, and he would not put his name to it.

Jon asked whether the extra was a guess. Master Wyl said the coffer and the digging were a guess and the arch was a price, and that he would take the arch at a fixed sum and the coffer at a sum he would account for by the day, and that if he found rock at eight feet instead of gravel at twelve Lord Stark would have the difference back. Jon took it on those terms. The excess over the roll's price fell within the roll's allowance for difficult sites and was written there. Cerwyn's four parts in ten were to be sand from his own river bank, gravel from his pit, lime burned in his kiln and his tenants' labor at the coffer, all at the book's rates, and Cerwyn's maester was to keep the tally of them on the south bank while Winterfell's clerk kept it on the north, and the two tallies were to be compared each month.

The coffer went in during the ninth moon at low water, and the digging reached gravel at eleven feet and a half, which was Master Wyl's best news of the year. The raft and the concrete bed were laid before the river rose. The centering timber was felled in Cerwyn's wood and stacked to season over the winter, and the mason went back to White Harbor to cut and dress the voussoirs through the cold moons by his own fire, at his own charge until they were delivered, which Jon had insisted on.

### The square

The market square was finished on the tenth day of the tenth moon, in dressed setts laid in courses on sand, and the clerk's comparison of the gate tally with the paviour's bill found sixty loads of setts on the bill that had never passed the gate.

Jon heard it in the solar with the master paviour, his Winter Town foreman, the gate sergeant and the clerk. The foreman had kept the paviour's count of loads at the square. The sergeant had kept the gate's. The two counts agreed to the load for the first six weeks and then parted, by five and six loads a week, until the end. The foreman said the gate sergeant counted badly. The sergeant said he counted every cart that came through the gate and had done for eleven years and would be glad to be shown one he had missed. The master paviour said his foreman had been with him four years and was honest.

Jon asked the foreman where the sixty loads of setts were. He said they were in the square. Jon had the square measured that afternoon by the reeve and the carpenter with the knotted line, and it took what the gate had passed, with a dozen loads over that were stacked in a lane for repairs, and no more. The foreman had nothing more to say. Jon dismissed him from the works, fined him the value of the sixty loads, which he could not pay and which was entered against him, and told the master paviour that the sum came off his own last payment because the foreman had been his man and the bill had been under his name. The master paviour said that was hard. Jon said it was the price of saying a man was honest without counting his loads, and that he would be glad to have the master back for the lanes next year if he came with a different foreman.

### The road

He rode out with the Barrowton-road patrol in the second week of the tenth moon, eight men under Donnel, for four days, because the road poles were in and he wanted to see them with snow on the ground rather than hear that they were there.

The first snow of the year had come in the first week and lay a hand deep on the road and deeper in the hollows. The poles stood every fifty yards, tarred at the foot, with the tops split and a bunch of straw in the split, and in the hollows where the road could not be told from the field they stood out like a fence. Donnel said the patrol had ridden the road in snow for six years and had lost the way in the Rills hollow every year, and had not lost it this year, and that the men had taken to calling the poles by the names of the sergeants who had been lost there.

They slept the second night at the patrol post at the Rills crossing, a turf-walled house with a hearth and a stable, with the men on the benches and Jon on the one bed, which he took because refusing it would have embarrassed everyone. He ate what the men ate and listened to them talk about the Barrowton wolves, which had come back to the river pastures and taken a yearling calf, and about the horse-wardens, whom the patrol now knew by name. On the third day they met Dustin's road gang.

Lord Dustin had begun his twelve miles from the Barrowton end with thirty tenants and eight wagons, and had three miles gravelled by the time Jon rode over them, laid on the old road where it was sound and on a new line where it was not, with ditches on both sides that the Barrowton reeve was proud of. The reeve had Stark's advance of coin for the gravel carts and Dustin's own men for the labor, and kept both tallies in one book, which Jon had the clerk copy later and found honest. The reeve said his lord had told him the road was to be done before the spring floods or he would hear about it, and that his lord had also said the Stark was not to be shown the bad mile at the ford until it was fixed, and then said he supposed he should not have said that, and Jon rode on and looked at the bad mile.

### Answers

The Dreadfort answered on the note in the eighth moon, in the lord's own hand this time and in four lines: the note was his, the debt was his, two thousand a year was more than the Dreadfort could spare with its own works wanting, and he would pay fifteen hundred a year for four years from the fourth moon of 127. Jon accepted it. Lord Dustin's answer came in the seventh moon, late and blunt: the regent had given him the money to clear White Harbor and he had never thought of it as a loan, but the note said what it said and a Dustin paid what a Dustin sealed; he would pay half in coin at the instalments and half in horses from his own herds, at the book price, because coin was what Barrowton had least of and horses were what it had most, and Winterfell's patrols rode worse horses than his herdsmen. Jon took the horses, to be valued by the stable master as they came and counted against the note at the book price.

Lord Umber paid his tenth-moon instalment in full and sent nothing for the sixteen hundred, and his letter said his four granaries had their frames up on Winterfell's timber and he would send the arrears when the roofs were on. Jon let the letter lie. Ryswell paid his tenth-moon instalment in full and wrote, for the first time, that a murrain had gone through his horse herds in the spring and taken the coin he would have sent in the fourth moon, and asked until the fourth moon of 127 for the eleven hundred he was short. Jon granted it in writing and told the clerk to carry it as owed.

Lord Desmond agreed to the measures. Thirty sets would be made at White Harbor by his scale-smiths against the Winterfell standard, with the stores officer's deputy sitting with the smith and six White Harbor merchants putting their marks on the receipt, and he would send the sets up with the spring grain carts. Hornwood's charcoal burners fired their two kilns in the eighth moon. The first gave ninety gallons and the second, after they had relined the pit, a hundred and twenty. ## Turn 6 | Thirtieth day of the second moon, 127 AC | Evening

Elapsed: A hundred and twenty days, from the evening of the thirtieth day of the tenth moon of 126 to the evening of the thirtieth day of the second moon of 127.

### Fire

The night watch on the south wall saw the glow in the second hour after midnight on the eighteenth day of the eleventh moon and rang the gate bell, and Jon was in the yard with his boots unlaced before the second peal.

It was in the low lane behind the old market, where the chandler rendered his tallow in a shed against the back wall of his house, and by the time the first garrison men came down the approach at a run the shed was gone and the chandler's thatch was burning and the thatch of the house beyond it had caught at the eaves. The lane was eleven feet wide. The wind was from the west, which was toward the two new warehouses and the market hall.

The reeve had the fire point at the lane's end open before the garrison arrived: the cask unlidded, the ten buckets off their pegs, the ladder and the hooks down. He had thirty people in a line from the cask to the chandler's house passing buckets, and the cask was half empty, and the bucket line was doing what bucket lines did to a burning thatch, which was nothing. Jon took the line off the burning house and put it on the next one, wetting the thatch on the windward side, and put the ladder and the hooks on the house beyond that. Four garrison men went up the ladder and the hooks went into the thatch at the ridge and the men on the ropes below pulled it down into the lane in smoking slabs, and the roof timbers stood bare and did not burn, having nothing on them to burn. The second cask was brought from the square on a handcart and the line went on from it. The chandler's house went, and the two beyond it, where the thatch had caught before the hooks came. The fourth house kept its bare rafters. The warehouses, forty yards downwind, took sparks on their shingles all night and a man on each roof with a wet sack, and in the morning the shingles were scorched in a dozen places and whole.

Nobody died. The chandler burned both hands pulling his rendering copper out of the shed and was the first man treated in the new infirmary, by the reeve's wife, there being no chirurgeon yet. Three families lodged in the winter hall, which had been built for strangers and took its own townspeople first.

Jon held the inquiry in the market hall the next morning with the reeve, the watch officer and the chandler's neighbors. The copper had been left on the fire with the shed door shut. The chandler said he had banked it. Jon ordered the three houses rebuilt by their owners at their own charge, as the custom was, but with shingle and not thatch, and the lane widened by the width of the burned shed, which the chandler could not have back; a second cask at each point within sixty paces of the warehouses, the hall and the weavers' houses; and a rule, cried in the square, that no rendering, brewing or forge fire in Winter Town was to be left banked in a closed shed overnight, on a fine of the fire casks' price. The reeve said the townspeople would hate it. Jon said they would hate it less than the chandler's neighbors hated him.

### The cold moons

The winter hall kept sixty people through the twelfth and first moons, carters caught by the snow, the three burned-out families, a party of Umber men who had brought their lord's grain to the Hornwood landing and could not get home, and the usual drunks, under a keeper who had been the gaoler at the east gate and knew how to count heads at night. The chirurgeon came in the first moon, a barber-surgeon called Lew who had served eleven years on Lord Desmond's ships and had been put ashore for drinking, which Lord Desmond's letter said plainly and Jon took on its own terms. He set a carter's leg in his first week and lost a child to a winter cough in his second, and kept both in a book.

The school kept its fourteen boys. Two could cut a true tally by midwinter and read it back; the rest could not, and Hobb said that was the ordinary proportion and that the two were the reeve's son and a tenant's boy from the Upper White Knife, and that he would have the tenant's boy in the works account by summer if Jon would let him.

Lord Umber's letter came in the twelfth moon. One of his four granaries had sagged under the first heavy snow, the ridge had gone down a foot and the gable had split, and eighty quarters of barley in it were wet and spoiling, and it was Winterfell's green timber that had done it, and he would send his sixteen hundred dragons when Winterfell had sent sound wood. Jon showed the letter to the carpenter, who had chosen and marked the timber. The carpenter said the timber had been winter-felled oak and pine, sound and seasoned the one year the roll allowed, and that no sound timber in the world would hold a roof pitched as flat as the Last Hearth pitched its roofs under four feet of snow, and that Umber's own carpenter had built the roof. Jon wrote to Lord Umber that his carpenter would ride north at the thaw to look at the four roofs with Umber's carpenter beside him and say before both of them what had failed, and that until then the sixteen hundred was owed and the roof was Umber's. He did not expect the letter to be welcome and it was not answered.

Benjen had a cough in the twelfth moon that went to his chest, and the maester bled him and sat with him and he mended. Bennard was told at the month's end, as agreed, that his son had been sick and was well. Margaret's letters came and went through the maester and said nothing the maester thought Jon needed to read.

### The fair

The fair opened on the fifteenth day of the second moon with the market square paved under it and the hall roofed over it and nobody certain whether anyone would come.

They came. Cerwyn's tenants came first because they were nearest, over the new bridge, and then Tallhart's and Hornwood's and the Winter Town people themselves, and on the third day the Norrey came down the Flint's stone track with Arra and the Wull and two hundred sheep and forty packs of hill wool, which no clan had ever brought to a Stark fair in anyone's memory, because there had never been a reason to. Berrick came up from White Harbor with salt, wine, iron and three carts of Braavosi goods, and brought with him a Braavosi called Orro who had woven in Braavos for twenty years and had been sent north by the house Lord Desmond had written to, to see whether the North's wool was worth his time. Karstark's steward came with the first yarn from the Karhold spinning house, which the five independent weavers bought before noon. Dustin did not come but sent his steward with twelve hundred and fifty dragons in a chest and thirty horses from the river herds, which the stable master walked, trotted, looked in the mouth and valued one by one at the book price for a patrol horse, and wrote against the note.

The weighhouse decided more quarrels than the fair court did. The sealed set on its chain was used eleven hundred times in the fortnight by the weighmaster's count, and three times a day somebody's own weights were put beside it and found light, and most of those quarrels ended at the scale. The fair court under the steward sat each afternoon in the hall and heard fourteen matters in the fortnight: nine on weights and measures, two purses cut, one horse sold lame, one Bolton man and one Norrey man who had fought over a sheep that belonged to neither, and a Hornwood chapman caught with a false stone weight whom the court fined and whose goods it held until the fine was paid. Jon sat on the last day to hear the appeals, of which there were two, and upheld the court in both.

He had the lords who had come to supper on the fourth night: Cerwyn, Tallhart, Hornwood, the Norrey and the Wull at one table in the great hall, and the mead and the castle's ale, and Berrick and Orro at the end because Jon wanted them to hear the lords and the lords to hear them. Tallhart wanted his other three bridges, or the one that his tenants asked about most, and Jon said the one, from the public roll, if his maester would swear the site. Hornwood had six kilns and two hundred gallons of tar in barrels and wanted a second buyer in case Lord Desmond's shipwrights tired of him, and Berrick said he would not tire. The Norrey asked what a stone bridge cost, and was told, and said the hills would have poles. Orro said, through Berrick, that northern wool would never make broadcloth and would make a grey that sold in every port of the narrow sea if it were fulled by a mill and finished by someone who knew how, and that he would stay two years at twenty dragons a year and his keep, which was six times what a weaver got and about what a weaving master got in Braavos. Jon said he would not marry this year, nor the next, and would say the same to every lord at this table and every lord who was not at it, and that Lord Cerwyn should hear it from him before he heard it from anyone else. Cerwyn said he had heard it, and drank, and asked Tallhart about his bridge.

The fair's costs, the six watchmen, two porters, the weighmaster and the court's clerk, were twenty dragons. Jon said it was, at the rate the Crown took of everything else, and that the clerk was to write to the king's treasurer that a new fair had been held and its yield, because he would rather the Crown read it in his own letter than in anyone else's.

On the evening of the thirtieth the running count stood at four hundred and six thousand one hundred and forty dragons, with Dustin's first coin in it and the fair's. The carpenter was to ride north for the Last Hearth at the thaw. Jon told the steward that he would go north himself after him, by the Dreadfort and the Last Hearth and Karhold and back by the Hornwood, as soon as the Acorn Water centering was up and he had seen it, because the Dreadfort's first fifteen hundred was due in the fourth moon and he wanted to be in the Dreadfort's hall when it was counted.

## Turn 7 | Thirtieth day of the sixth moon, 127 AC | Evening

### The spring

The voussoirs came up from White Harbor on the first carts after the thaw, forty-one dressed stones to the cart and eleven carts, each stone numbered on its bed with the mason's chisel, and Master Wyl behind them on a mule. He had the south abutment up on the gravel by the end of the fourth moon, in coursed hill stone with a lime core, and the centering frames made on the south bank from Cerwyn's seasoned timber: four trusses the shape of the arch's underside, to be stood on the abutments when the river fell and the voussoirs laid over them from both springers until they met. Jon rode north on the eighth day of the fourth moon with twelve men, the carpenter having gone ahead to the Last Hearth a week before. Before he went he did a thing he had not done before and gave his key of the vault to the maester, with a sheet in his own hand saying that the maester held it for the counting of the fourth-moon instalment and the sending of the Crown's share and for nothing else, and that the steward and the clerk were to count as if Jon stood at the table. ### The Dreadfort

The Dreadfort stood on its hill above the Weeping Water with the flayed man on every tower, six days' ride east through country that got poorer as the road got worse. The Lord of the Dreadfort met him in the yard, a lean man of fifty with a quiet voice who had never raised it in Jon's hearing and, by Cregan's memory, had never needed to. He gave Jon the best rooms and the best supper and the first fifteen hundred dragons of the note on the second morning, in his own hall, counted by his steward onto his own table before Jon and Jon's clerk, in silver stags mostly and some gold, with the count cut into two sticks. He said nothing of what the money had been for in 122 and Jon did not ask him.

He had done a thing Jon had not expected. His steward had been at the Winter Town fair in the second moon and had walked through the two new granaries behind the castle with the stores officer, who showed anyone who asked, and had come home and told his lord, and the lord had built two of the same at the Dreadfort over the winter from his own timber at his own charge, with the floors on piers and the eaves wide, and had told Winterfell nothing about it. He showed Jon them on the second afternoon without any particular pride, as he might have shown him a new well, and said the one thing he had not been able to copy was the stores officer, and asked whether the boys in Winterfell's school would be for hire when they could count. Jon said they would be Stark's clerks first. The lord said that was what he had supposed.

He named his four villages for the wells, at last, and Jon had the clerk write the agreement there, Stark's six parts and the Dreadfort's four in labor, with the lord's seal on it before supper.

On the last night, with the hall emptied and the two of them and the maesters at the high table, the Lord of the Dreadfort asked what Winterfell would do if the king died and the succession were disputed, which he said everyone from here to the Wall was asking and nobody from here to the Wall would ask Lord Stark to his face. Jon said that Winterfell would keep the North's oath and the North's peace, in that order, and that the Dreadfort would hear what that meant when it meant something. The lord said that was an answer he would have given himself. They parted with every courtesy and nothing settled, which Jon had expected, and which was the Dreadfort's way of being on good terms.

### The Last Hearth

The Last Hearth was seven days further north, through the Lonely Hills and across the Last River, and Lord Umber met him at the ford on a horse the size of a plough ox, roaring.

The roaring was about the roof. He had the four granaries lined up below the hall on the river flat, three of them sound and one of them with its ridge down a foot and its gable split and its spoiled barley heaped outside it under a sail, and he had his carpenter there and Winterfell's carpenter there and he wanted it said in front of both of them whose fault it was.

Winterfell's carpenter said it in front of both of them. He had been up on the roof two days. The frames were Winterfell's timber and were sound; he had cut into a post and showed the heart. The purlins were not Winterfell's timber; they were the Last Hearth's own pine, cut that autumn and green, and they had bowed under the first snow and let the ridge down. And the pitch was the Last Hearth's pitch, which was flat because Umber roofs had always been flat, because they were turf, and a plank roof under four feet of snow was not a turf roof. Umber's carpenter said the lord had told him to use his own purlins because Winterfell's were too short. Lord Umber said he had said nothing of the kind, and then said that if he had, the carpenter should have said they were green, and then said nothing for a while.

Jon settled it standing in front of the split gable. ### Karhold

### The Hornwood

He came back by the Hornwood, four days west and south through the forest, and Lord Hornwood walked him through the six kilns on the north side where the old pine stood: clay-lined pits with a channel at the bottom and a barrel under the channel, each with its burner, the forest around them smelling of tar for a mile. Lord Desmond was a large man who had grown larger, with two sons of twenty and seventeen who had their father's size and their mother's manners, and he kept Jon ten days and showed him everything. The warehouse floors Stark rented stood on the quay with Stark's grain in one and Hornwood's tar and Winter Town's grey in the other, under a Manderly watchman and a Stark tally. The scale-smiths who had made the thirty measure sets worked in a lane behind the fish market, and the oldest of them showed Jon the Winterfell standard they had kept a copy of and said it was the best weight that had ever come out of the North, which Jon took as the compliment it was meant for. The customs house on the quay took the Crown's part of every cargo under a royal officer who bowed to Jon and told him nothing, which was his office.

Elric was sixteen, a page in Lord Desmond's household, tall for a Stark and quiet, and he came to Jon in the solar the New Castle had given him and stood as a page stood and answered what he was asked. He was well. He was taught letters by the maester and arms by the master-at-arms and rode with the lord's sons. He asked after his father and his brother and was told what the maester told Bennard each month, and he said thank you. He did not ask to come home. On the last day he asked whether his mother had been let see his father, and Jon said she had, in the tenth moon, and Elric said he was glad of it and went back to his duties, and that was all that passed between the Lord of Winterfell and the son of the man who had tried to kill him, and Jon thought it was enough for a page of sixteen and perhaps for both of them.

The Braavosi came on the fourth day. The house that had sent Orro north had a factor in White Harbor, a thin man with ink on his fingers who had seen the second season's grey and wanted ten thousand yards a year of it from the spring of 128 at three stags and a half the yard, fulled by the mill and finished under Orro's hand and not otherwise, and would take nothing foot-fulled. Jon said the mill would run in the third moon and the cloth would be what Orro passed and that he would sign for what the looms could make and not for ten thousand, which Orro said was eight thousand in a good year with the nine weavers and the five who bought yarn. The factor signed for eight. Berrick stood at the table and said nothing, having been bettered by his own introduction, and Lord Desmond laughed at him.

Lord Desmond gave him two things more. The king's treasurer's receipts for both of 127's chests, with the treasurer's clerk's note on the autumn one that the fair's thirty-two dragons had been received and that the Crown noted a new fair at Winter Town and claimed its part of it, which Jon had expected and was glad to have in writing. And a cog of Manderly's that would carry a Stark man and the old letter to Skagos in the spring when the ice let it, if the Skagosi would let a Stark man land, which Desmond did not promise.

He gave Jon the news of the south on the last night, over the fish, with his sons sent away. The king had been sick again through the autumn and the Hand governed in his name; the queen's party held the court and the princess held Dragonstone; the Velaryon fleet held the Gullet and asked every ship that passed it whose it was; and the Hand's clerks and the princess's both wrote to White Harbor to ask what its ships carried and where. Desmond answered both the same, which was that White Harbor's ships carried what their owners paid for. Then he asked, as the Dreadfort had asked, what Winterfell intended, and Jon gave him the Dreadfort's answer, the oath and the peace. Desmond asked, which was more than the Dreadfort had done, the oath to whom. Jon said to the king while the king lived, and that the rest was not yet a question. Desmond said he would hold White Harbor's ships to the same.

He raised the other matter over the last cup, as every lord raised it. His lady had a niece. Jon said what he had said at Winterfell's table and would say again, and Desmond nodded as a man nods who has done his duty by his wife, and then asked about Elric, who was sixteen and would be a man in two years, and whether Winterfell would hear a Manderly match for him when the time came. Jon said that Elric's marriage waited on his majority and on the oaths he had sworn, and that when it did not wait any longer Lord Desmond would be heard before anyone. That was true and it was also nothing, and both of them knew it, and they parted on it.

He was home on the twenty-fourth day of the twelfth moon with the ice on the Acorn Water bridge's parapets and the road poles standing out of a foot of snow all the way from the river to the gate.

### The cold moons

The tannery's first forty hides came out of the pits on the ninth day of the second moon of 128, after fifteen moons under lime and bark, and the journeyman from Barrowton laid them on the shed floor and went over every one with his hands and said thirty-six were leather and four were not, and that four in forty was what a new yard gave and one in forty was what an old one gave. The thirty-six were sold at the fair to Barrowton's saddlers, who knew what they were buying, except for six that the stable master kept for Winterfell's harness. Jon had the works book show what the yard had cost and what the thirty-six hides had fetched, and the one figure was eleven times the other, and the journeyman said that was what a tannery looked like in its second year and that he would say the same about its third.

The mill stood ready with its wheel hung and its stocks and hammers framed, and waited on the ice, which held the race until the first week of the third moon. The winter hall had ninety people in it at the turn of the year and the keeper counted them every night by the light of the fire casks' lanterns. Lew lost a carter who fell from the winter hall's loft drunk and broke his neck, and set two arms and a collarbone, and treated a child's fever with something from a Braavosi jar he had bought with his own money, and the child lived, and he wrote all of it in his book in a hand the maester said was better than his own.

The school had twenty boys. Four of them, the reeve's son and three tenants' boys, could cut, read and cast a tally by the second moon, and Hobb brought them to the solar and had each cast the account of a week of the works before Jon, and Jon took two into the works account under the clerk and sent two to the receivers of the Upper White Knife and the kingsroad south as tally clerks at the book's pay, which was the first thing the school had made that could be counted.

Benjen asked, through the maester, for books, and was given three. Bennard was told at the month's ends that his son was well.

### The second fair

The fair opened on the fifteenth day of the second moon with a hundred and forty stalls, forty more than the year before, and with the winter hall full before the first day and the brewer's widow's two baths taking a penny more than their price from people who did not argue.

The clans came down together this time, the Norrey, the Wull, the Liddle and the Harclay with their sheep and their wool, and behind them a man of the Knott's with four ponies, who said nothing to anyone, bought twenty axes from a Winter Town smith with hill silver, and went home. Arra came with her father and stayed at the castle and not the winter hall, and said she would stay through the shearing this year and go up after it, which was the other way about from the year before and which she did not explain.

Glover came himself, for the first time, and brought Brandon Stark with him, nineteen now and a squire in Glover's household, who had his father's face and had not been in Winterfell since the twelfth moon of 125. Brandon asked Jon, in the solar on the second evening and alone, whether he might come home, or if not home then into Winterfell's household in any place Jon chose, because he had sworn the oath and kept it and Deepwood Motte was not his country. Jon said not yet. He said that the oath had two more years to run before Brandon was a man under it, that Glover's house was a good house and had been chosen for him for that reason, and that when the two years were done he would hear Brandon again and give him an answer that was not this one, and that it would not be Winterfell. Brandon said he had expected that and had wanted to have asked, which was what his mother had said in the same room, and Jon did not tell him so.

Lord Cerwyn, at the lords' supper, let it be known across the table that his daughter was betrothed to Lord Hornwood's heir and would be wed at the harvest, and looked at Jon while he said it, and Jon wished them joy and meant it, because it was a marriage he had neither made nor stopped and it bound two houses that were both his, and that was the kind of marriage he had said he wanted. Hornwood had sold six hundred gallons of tar in the year and asked for nothing. Tallhart's second bridge was done. Dustin's steward came with a thousand dragons in coin and fifty horses and asked that the note be cleared on the spot and the paper burned before him, because his lord did not like paper, and Jon had the clerk cast the sum, which came out, and burned the note in the solar hearth with Dustin's steward and the maester watching, and gave the steward a receipt for the ashes.

The fair court sat every afternoon and heard twenty-two matters, and one of them was Winterfell's own. The receiver of the kingsroad north holding, who had held it eleven years and under Bennard for six of them, had been taking the tenants' grain rent at his own measure since before anyone could remember. A tenant who had carried his rent to the weighhouse instead, out of spite, because the receiver had refused him a well, had it measured there against the sealed set, and the receiver's quarter was a quarter and a half-bushel over, and had been for eleven years. Jon heard it himself on the last day with the receiver's own measure on the table beside the sealed one. The receiver said his measure had been his father's. Jon said then his father had been a thief too, and dismissed him, and had his goods sold for what the tenants of the holding could prove they had overpaid in the last three years, which was all they could prove, and gave the holding's tally to one of Hobb's boys until a receiver could be found. The steward said afterward that it would be the first of several. Jon said he had bought thirty-six sets of measures for exactly that reason and had wondered what was taking so long.

The reeve's tally at the close put the goods sold in the fortnight at thirty-one thousand two hundred dragons' worth, and the one part in a hundred at three hundred and twelve collected at the scale, with the fair's costs at twenty-four, which was the six watchmen and two more. Two Braavosi had come up with Berrick to look at the wool on the hoof and had bought none, and had bought grey, and had asked about the mill.

The ice went out of the race on the sixth day of the third moon and the millwright opened his sluice and the wheel turned and the stocks lifted and fell on the first bolt of unfulled grey, and Orro stood over the trough with the fuller who had done it by foot for two years and said that this was what fulling was, and the fuller said he had always said so.

On the evening of the fifteenth the running count stood at six hundred and twenty thousand and seventy dragons, Dustin's last coin in it and the fair's. The roll had taken seventeen thousand eight hundred and fifty-seven dragons in all, the winter having taken little, and stood committed for about four thousand four hundred more, the castle's grain yard and stable court let for the spring at the reeve's measure, the lanes to finish, the Hornwood wharf's deck, the first stage of the Flint of Flint's Finger's fish stations, which he had applied for at last after two years of watching Locke's, and a granary at Widow's Watch. The steward asked whether the fair's two hundred and eighty-eight dragons were to go to the fund or the vault. Jon said the vault, and the Crown's part with the spring chests, and that the fund was for building and the fair was for proving the buildings had been worth it.
