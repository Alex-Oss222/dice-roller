The clerk was still speaking when Jon became aware of the room. There was an account book before him, open at a heading dated 126 AC. The man beside the table wore a bunch of keys at his belt. Jon did not know him.

The body in the chair was Cregan Stark's. Jon remembered his own life through 487 AC, but none of the conversation that had put these papers in front of him.

“Shall I leave these with you, Lord Cregan?”

The clerk pulled the loose sheets together, knocked their edges against the table and laid a narrow board over them. He passed cord beneath the board, crossed it twice and tied the bundle. A split in the cord caught on the corner. He cut that length away and started again.

“That is everything,” he said.

A page came through the door with another bundle. The clerk took it from him.

The man with the keys waited until both had gone. A guard remained outside the open door.

“Your uncle has asked to see you. He wants no clerk present.”

He set a separate folded sheet beside the accounts. It was addressed to Lord Cregan Stark. Bennard's name stood beneath a short request for an audience.

“He and the boys are still confined. I've changed nothing there.”

Jon could remember administering prisons and hearing petitions. He could not remember this man's name, or what Cregan had last told him to do. The steward had brought his own writing case and was taking out a knife to mend the point of his quill.

“He says it is family business. I told the guard I'd bring his request.”

The steward laid the quill beside the account book. He had not sent for Bennard, and the guard had received no instruction to move the prisoners.

“Privately? No. He has no right to speak with me privately. Station two guards at the door. Send for Arra Norrey before you bring him.”

The steward put away his quill and went to give the instructions.

Jon untied the bundle. He laid the loose sheets beside the account book, separated the petitions from the receipts, and arranged the dated papers in order. Several had writing on the reverse. He turned each over before setting it aside.

The steward’s endorsements supplied his name: Walton.

Most of the business was ordinary. A miller sought payment for grinding grain already delivered to the kitchens. The smith wanted approval to replace worn ironwork on a gate. The account book recorded receipts from Winterfell’s own lands alongside issues of fodder, food, candles, and wages. Some payments had been authorised by Bennard before his confinement; newer requests awaited Cregan’s approval.

Jon checked the additions and followed the carried sums back through the preceding pages. They agreed. Whether the grain and coin agreed with the writing would require a different examination.

Arra arrived while he was reading the household duty lists. Walton brought her in, then withdrew to arrange the prisoner’s escort.

She pulled a chair towards the table.

“What does he want?”

“He hasn’t said.”

Jon explained the work he wanted done: who could be relied upon, who remained attached to Bennard, and what those attachments amounted to. She was to use the retainers who had accompanied her from the clans rather than entrust the inquiry to the household guards.

Arra listened without interrupting. When he finished, she looked towards the door.

“Harl and Donnel came with me. I trust them. Harl can keep quiet, and Donnel has been spending enough time with the grooms to know some of their business.”

She drew one of the duty lists closer.

“They’re not going to get honest answers by asking men whether they favour your uncle. I’ll have them begin with who carried his messages, who visited him, who still speaks of getting him out. Then we can see what agrees.”

Her finger stopped beside a name.

“Some of these men served your father as well. I’ll want more than someone saying Bennard gave them their place.”

She would begin within Winterfell. Anything extending to the households beyond it would take messengers, journeys, and more time. She made no promise of a complete reckoning that day.

Arra went out to speak to her men. Jon returned to the papers.

The keeper’s latest note reported all four prisoners receiving their meals and requested fresh bedding. A copy of the confinement instructions required permission before admitting visitors. Nothing in the packet showed who had actually carried the food or stood every watch. The duty lists named the men assigned, not whether each had been present.

Several petitions still addressed Bennard as the person expected to settle them. Their dates preceded his imprisonment. Jon found no fresh report of an armed gathering against Winterfell, but there were no fresh reports from much of the North at all.

He read through the last sheet before setting the bundle aside. No payment had been approved, no petitioner answered, and no officer dismissed.

Arra returned before Bennard was summoned.

“They’ve started,” she said. “I told them to bring what they hear to me before they accuse anyone.”

At Jon’s request, a household armsman brought a serviceable sword from the armoury. Jon drew it far enough to examine the edge and point, returned it to its scabbard, and fastened the belt where the hilt would remain clear of the chair. Arra had a sheathed knife at her own belt. She took a seat on his side of the table.

The escort arrived with Bennard.

At the threshold, the guards checked beneath his outer garment, around his waistband, and down the tops of his boots. They found no weapon. One took position on either side of the doorway, inside the chamber. Bennard was brought no farther than the opposite edge of the table.

He looked at Arra, then at the guards.

“I asked to speak with you alone.”

“You may speak here.”

Bennard remained standing.

“I ask nothing for myself. It is my sons I came about.”

His tunic was clean, though creased from wear. He had been shaved badly beneath one ear.

“They took their orders from me. You know that. Whatever you intend for me, hear them before you decide they must share it.”

He glanced towards the doorway again.

“Let them come before your household and swear obedience. Give them duties where you can watch them. They are still Starks.”

Arra said nothing.

“And I want my own servant attending me again,” Bennard continued. “My writing chest returned, too. You can have it searched.”

He had offered no names of supporters, no admission beyond giving his sons their orders, and no explanation of why he had insisted upon privacy.

“Agree to that, and I will put my submission in writing. Have it read in the hall. The men who served me can hear from my own words that they are to obey you.”

Bennard waited across the table. His sons remained confined, and Arra’s men had not yet returned with a report.

“An admission, then.”

Jon left Bennard standing across the table.

“I was Lord of Winterfell from the day my father died. All the North was mine to govern, from the mountains to the bogs. You were meant to be his faithful brother and safeguard what belonged to his son. Not keep it for yourself.”

“You were thirteen. Someone had to govern.”

Bennard had been entitled to govern during Cregan’s minority. Cregan had nevertheless inherited the lordship, and his uncle had continued holding power after he came of age.

“Your life for your sons,” Jon said. “I will not kill them or raise a hand against them unless they move against me first. They will not go to the Wall. They will be kept comfortably, but they will hold no lands and enter no other lord’s service.”

Bennard looked towards Arra. She remained seated.

“You will put your admission in writing,” Jon continued. “Bennard Stark, a traitor to his kin, his family, and the North. It will be sent throughout my lands and read aloud. You will die, and I will carry out the sentence myself. That is the mercy I offer because you are my father’s brother.”

“I asked nothing for myself.”

He had asked for his servant back and the return of his writing chest.

“You would make yourself a kinslayer over this?”

Jon knew the accusation would not disappear merely because the killing followed a sentence. Men who accepted Bennard’s guilt could still condemn the nephew who killed him.

“You have tonight and tomorrow night to consider it. On the third night, I will come for your answer.”

“Then put the promise to my sons in writing as well. Under your seal. Let them have something more than what you tell me in this room.”

Jon signalled to the guards.

One opened the door. The other moved beside Bennard and directed him towards it. Bennard went without struggling, but he had neither accepted the bargain nor agreed to write the confession. His demand for a sealed undertaking remained unanswered.

Arra waited until the guards had taken him down the passage.

“You have given him two nights,” she said. “His sons will spend them wondering what he chooses.”

Jon asked her to walk with him.

***

They entered the godswood together and left the path leading towards the heart tree. Jon sought a secluded stretch of ground he remembered from his own childhood, near a low rise within the enclosing walls.

The trees did not all stand where he remembered them. Beyond the rise, however, the ground dipped into a hollow screened from the busier paths. He could recognise the shape of the place without possessing a single memory of Cregan having walked there. His own memories had survived the transfer; Cregan’s private recollections had not come with them.

Arra stopped beside a pine.

“Why here?”

“I need to tell you the truth. I am not Cregan Stark.”

She watched him for a moment.

“Don’t.”

“My name is Jon Stark. I was raised as Jon Snow. I grew up in Winterfell, but not in this time. This morning I found myself here, in his body.”

Arra moved away from the tree. There was more space between them now.

“Is this something Bennard has done?”

“I don’t know what caused it.”

“Where is Cregan?”

“I don’t know.”

She looked back towards the path they had left. No one was visible between the trunks.

“You speak with his voice.”

Jon explained what he could: he remembered his own life, not Cregan’s conversations or private history. He did not know whether Cregan could be recovered, or what had happened to him. He offered no explanation he could not support.

“I am telling you because you are his friend. You knew him before any of this. If anyone here would try to help him, it would be you.”

“And this morning you sent for me to find out which men would obey you.”

“Yes. I still ask you to keep watch on Bennard’s men. But you can choose whether to help me. Take a fortnight. At the end of it, I would also hear what the inquiry has found.”

Arra rubbed a patch of resin from her sleeve with her thumb.

“I don’t know what to believe.”

She scraped at it again, unsuccessfully, and stopped.

“Harl and Donnel can keep asking their questions. They were doing that for Cregan’s safety. That hasn’t changed.”

She would not promise her allegiance to Jon. Nor would she promise to carry the investigation wherever he might later direct it.

“I won’t tell them what you have said. Not today. But I want to speak to you again before you put his uncle to the sword.”

When they returned to the main path, she did not take his arm.

“I’m going to my rooms.”

She left him there. Jon had told her his identity, but she had not said she believed him.

The maester was sorting correspondence when Jon entered his workroom. He moved a tray of used sealing wax off the spare stool.

Jon requested a raven to the Citadel for another maester.

The man left his pen beside the inkpot.

“Am I being dismissed, my lord?”

“No. You served my uncle. I cannot simply trust you because you now answer to me. Another maester is needed. You will both work for me, and there will be enough work for both.”

“Your uncle gave the orders here. I carried them out.”

He waited, then drew a fresh sheet towards him.

“I will make the request. The Citadel must decide whom it can send.”

There was no assurance that another man would arrive soon, or that the new arrival would prove more trustworthy. Nevertheless, the maester began the letter.

Jon had him date it and read it back before sealing it.

“Written at Winterfell, on the twelfth day of the fourth moon, in the year one hundred and twenty-six after the Conquest.”

“And the dispatch?”

“This afternoon. We are perhaps two hours past noon.”

That supplied the calendar without Jon having to admit he did not know it. Bennard’s answer would fall on the night of the fourteenth. Arra’s fortnight would end on the twenty-sixth.

The maester made a copy in his register. He dusted the original, tipped the excess sand into its box, rolled the parchment and secured it in a small message tube. In the adjoining rookery, he selected a bird kept for Oldtown, checked the fastening, and carried it to the opening.

The raven went out over the roofs. Its departure did not yet mean the Citadel had received the request; such birds carried messages along the routes for which they had been trained.

Back in the workroom, Jon gave his instructions concerning Moat Cailin. He wanted the man responsible for the stronghold consulted, an inventory of the repairs it required, and an examination of what the coffers could support. The maester was to bring the results in seven days.

The maester entered the nineteenth beneath the instruction.

“I can bring you the accounts by then, with whatever information has reached us. I cannot ride to the Moat, examine its works, meet its keeper and return here inside seven days.”

The stronghold stood far to the south, at the northern edge of the Neck. It was not a holding that could be inspected on an afternoon’s ride from Winterfell.

“And I would need a mason with me,” he continued. “I can record a broken stair. I cannot tell you whether the wall carrying it is sound.”

He fetched a ledger from a shelf and opened it beside the correspondence register.

“For the money, I will need Walton and the treasury records. What is owed to you is not necessarily here to spend. We must count what is present and set against it the payments already promised.”

Jon’s examination that morning had shown that the written sums agreed. It had not counted the contents of the chests or established what a rebuilding programme would cost.

The maester began listing the documents he would require. Beside the Moat inquiry, he left room for the keeper’s answer and a separate account of an inspection. He did not fill either space with estimates.

“I will report on the nineteenth,” he said. “Anything I have not verified will be marked as such. But the journey needs settling. If I go myself, I will be absent longer than your deadline, and you will have no maester here until I return or the Citadel sends another.”

He turned the unfinished instruction towards Jon.

“A mason could inspect the works while I examine the accounts here. Or the keeper could be summoned to bring his reports to Winterfell. Neither is the same as my seeing the place.”

Walton gathered the people responsible for running Winterfell.

The closest equivalent to department heads was the castle’s **household officers**. The steward oversaw its business and accounts; the captain of guards arranged security and watches; the master-at-arms trained its fighting men; the master of horse managed the stables. The senior servants and craftsmen responsible for the kitchens, stores, kennels, and smithy had their own work and people to answer for. These were household offices, not appointments to a royal small council.

“They are all here,” Walton said.

The kennelmaster arrived halfway through the steward’s first report.

Jon heard each explain what fell under his charge, who answered to him, and what work was waiting. Walton’s clerk recorded the names and responsibilities. The first page filled before they reached the stores, and he turned it over rather than begin a second sheet.

The smith’s request for gate ironwork was still awaiting approval. The cook wanted the grain promised to the kitchens distinguished from grain already delivered. The master of horse reported the mounts needed for the maester’s party and what their absence would leave available.

Jon set out what he expected.

“I want an honest account of what each of you has, what is owed, and what is lacking. Give it to Walton. Keep the ordinary work going. Where something needs my decision, bring it to me before neglect makes it urgent.”

The clerk looked up.

“Those words, my lord?”

“The substance. We are not writing a proclamation.”

Jon turned to the captain of guards.

“The watches and the prisoners remain your responsibility. The existing restrictions stand. My uncle’s wishes are not orders.”

The captain acknowledged that.

For the other officers, Jon required the same plain accountability: supplies issued were to be recorded, unfinished work identified, and problems reported rather than concealed beneath a satisfactory total. He did not dismiss anyone, impose new dues, or authorise the larger expenditures still before him.

Walton asked whether those unable to write should bring their accounts to his clerk.

“Yes.”

By the end, the clerk had a working list of responsibilities and outstanding matters. It was not yet an inventory of the castle, much less proof of anyone’s loyalty.

The cook took his own tally back with him. The smith left the petition concerning the gate on the table.

Before departure on the thirteenth, the mason wrapped a plumb bob, measuring cord, and small tools in a length of sacking. A groom secured the bundle beside the maester’s document case. Donnel checked the packhorse’s straps, shifted a sack away from its spine, and had the load fastened again.

The maester left instructions for the rookery attendant and placed his unfinished correspondence with Walton’s clerk. There would be people to tend the birds and prepare letters, but no other maester had yet arrived.

Jon saw the party leave: the maester, Donnel, the mason, and a groom leading the packhorse.

The household work continued after them. Walton began collecting the requested accounts. Some could be checked within the walls; others depended upon receipts or people who were not at Winterfell. He kept those apart rather than entering promises as goods already held.

Harl continued his inquiries for Arra. Donnel’s conversations among the grooms had been handed over before he rode out, but there was no completed reckoning of Bennard’s adherents.

Jon waited.

On the evening of the fourteenth, Walton came to the working chamber.

“Your uncle has asked whether you still mean to come tonight.”

The maester had been gone little more than a day and a half. Bennard’s allotted two nights had passed.

Arra met Jon before he went to the prisoners.

“Harl has not finished,” she said. “I have nothing that settles which of Bennard’s sons did what.”

She waited until a passing servant had gone beyond the stair.

“And you told me you do not remember it yourself.”

Her voice was low enough not to carry to the guards.

“I want you to hear them before you kill their father. Whatever Bennard admits, hear them separately.”

She had made her request. She did not attempt to bar the passage, and Jon had not yet answered it.

Walton accompanied him to Bennard. Two guards remained at the doorway, within sight and hearing. The meeting was not private.

Bennard had been sitting on the edge of his bed. He stood when Jon entered.

“You have had your two nights,” Jon said.

“Yes.”

There was no written confession ready.

“I will admit that I kept the governance after you came of age,” Bennard continued. “I will name the orders I gave. What my sons did at my command, I will put to my own account.”

Walton opened his writing case.

“But I will not sign words that condemn them with me. And I will not buy their lives with a promise that dies when I do.”

Bennard looked directly at Jon.

“Put your undertaking beneath your seal. Their lives spared, no Wall, no lands, no service to another lord, as you said. Their maintenance provided. Let them hear you swear it before the heart tree.”

He had not asked again for his servant or his writing chest.

“Then I will set down what I did. You can have witnesses watch me write it.”

Walton laid out parchment but did not put pen to it. Bennard had offered admissions to specific acts; he had not agreed to the denunciation Jon demanded, and he still required a witnessed guarantee for his sons before signing anything.

“Will you give them that?” Bennard asked. The guards remained at the door while Walton waited beside the unused sheet.

Bennard’s sons came to the heart tree under escort. Walton named each as he read the undertaking: Benjen, Brandon, Elric.

They were promised their lives and maintenance from Winterfell’s household. They would not be sent to the Wall. They would hold no lands and enter no lord’s service. Jon would not move against them unless they first moved against him.

The agreement did not turn them into trusted retainers or give them leave to depart unnoticed. For the present, their confinement would be eased into guarded household lodging, with proper meals, clothing, fires, and access to one another.

Bennard listened to every provision.

“Read the part about their maintenance again.”

Walton did.

“Father?”

Bennard embraced him. Brandon came next, then Elric, who remained close to him after the others had stepped away.

Jon allowed them their time.

After the oath, Jon went to the training yard.

The master-at-arms found him a padded jack, gloves, and a practice sword. An armsman tightened the fastening at Jon’s shoulder, checked that the padding covered his collarbone, and handed him a plain helm.

Jon had not lost the technique and judgment acquired through his former life. What remained uncertain was their expression through Cregan’s reach, strength, conditioning, and recovery.

His first opponent was careful.

Too careful. The man showed his intended cut before moving and left Jon ample room to answer it.

“Strike properly.”

“I am, my lord.”

The next cut stopped short of an opening at Jon’s shoulder.

Jon lowered his sword.

The master-at-arms sent that man back to the watching line and came forward himself.

They began again.

Jon recognised the first invitation and left it alone. When the older man stepped in, Jon met his blade and turned the point aside. His intended answer went farther than he expected. He checked it, recovered, and took a blunt touch against the outside of his arm before he could close the line.

They separated.

The next exchange was shorter. Jon let the attack develop, moved only far enough to clear it, and brought his sword against the padded ribs.

The master-at-arms grunted.

“Again.”

By the time they changed opponents, Jon had stopped reaching for movements that depended upon his former body’s particular speed. Against the next armsman, he worked from a narrower stance and let the other man expend himself trying to force an opening. Jon’s answers became smaller and arrived earlier.

He did not go untouched. A late recovery earned him another knock, and his forearm began to tire before his judgment did. But the men facing him had stopped offering easy attacks. One asked for another bout after being caught twice by the same change of line.

When Jon finally removed the helm, the master-at-arms examined the mark on his sleeve.

“That will bruise.”

It did. It remained sore the following morning, though it did not prevent him using the arm.

Ghost had watched from beside the steps. The men gave him a wide passage when they went for water.

Two days later, Jon had Ice brought out.

The armourer laid the sheathed greatsword on a cleared bench. He removed the covering, inspected the fittings, and drew the blade far enough to check for moisture before passing it over.

Jon remembered being allowed to handle it as a boy under Eddard’s supervision. Ice was the same vast, two-handed Valyrian-steel weapon: dark, rippled, and much broader than an ordinary sword.

He took it in both hands.

The balance became apparent as soon as the point cleared the bench. He raised it slowly, turned it, and lowered it again. There was room for both hands on the grip, but its length demanded more space than the practice sword had.

The armourer opened the door onto the clear ground outside.

Jon worked through measured cuts without an opponent. He checked where the point passed, how much room a recovery needed, and what happened when he tried to bring it back too quickly. The blade was lighter than its dimensions suggested, but its size still governed the movement.

He adjusted his footing.

On the next cut, the sword travelled cleanly through the intended line and stopped where he meant it to stop. He repeated the movement, then changed direction and tested the recovery.

He did not take a live Valyrian-steel edge into a friendly bout.

Over the following days, he returned to it in short sessions. By the twenty-first, he could raise, deliver, and recover a full two-handed stroke without crowding himself or allowing the point to wander. His ordinary sword remained the more convenient weapon to wear around the castle.

After each session, the blade was wiped, inspected, and put away.

Arra came to the working chamber on the evening before the execution. Ghost lifted his head when she entered, then settled again.

Jon asked for her decision.

“You gave me until the twenty-sixth,” she said. “You will have the inquiry then. I can answer the other part now.”

She placed a folded sheet beside the accounts.

“I will help you. I will stay here, and I will keep what you told me to myself.”

Jon let her continue.

“That does not mean I understand it. The wolf knows you. I saw that. I still don’t know what happened to Cregan.”

She had not abandoned the hope of finding him. Nor had she decided that Jon’s account explained everything she had witnessed.

“I want that question kept open.”

On the sheet were two names Harl had brought her: Edrik, a gate serjeant, and Ronnel, one of the household clerks.

“Both speak well of Bennard. Harl heard Edrik say the castle was better run before the arrest. Ronnel thinks his sons have been badly used.”

Neither report established a plot. Harl had found no evidence that either man had carried a clandestine message since the confinement, and Arra would not label them loyal to Jon merely because they continued doing their work.

“Donnel might have learned more from the stables,” she added. “But you needed him on the road.”

The maester’s party had not returned. There was still no completed survey from Moat Cailin, and no second maester had arrived from the Citadel.

Arra looked towards the folded sheet.

“The confession is being read. So is your promise. Men are talking about both.”

“What are they saying?”

“Some say Bennard brought this upon himself. Some think you should have kept him confined. Harl heard the word *kinslayer* twice.”

She had already asked Jon to hear the sons before deciding. He had instead made and sworn his settlement.

“I will be there tomorrow,” she said. “I mean to hear you keep it.”

On the morning of the twenty-second, the guards brought Bennard into the godswood.

He wore a clean tunic. His wrists were bound for the walk, and the captain had them released once he was positioned beside the block. Two guards remained close enough to intervene.

Benjen had chosen to attend. His brothers had said their farewells and remained in their lodging.
