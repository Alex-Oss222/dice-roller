## Turn 12 | Thirtieth day of the first moon, 129 AC | Evening

Elapsed: Thirty days, from the evening of the thirtieth day of the twelfth moon of 128 to the evening of the thirtieth day of the first moon of 129.

### Walking it

He gave the first week of the moon to walking the roll's work with the people who had done it, before the account was read, so that when a column was read in the hall he would have seen what stood behind the number.

The grain yard on the third day, with the stores officer: six granaries in two rows on their stone piers, the first two weathered grey and the four from the summer still pale, the paving between them crowned to its gutters and the gutters running. He went into each. The stores officer had the heaps turned in the first week of every moon through the winter by Hobb's boys, and the boys' tallies hung on a nail inside each door, and the bottom of each heap was dry. In the old north granary the floor was on the ground as it had always been and the stores officer showed him the bottom sack, which was not dry, and said that was what a pier was for and that he could not say it plainer. Twenty thousand two hundred quarters under the castle's roofs; he had the figure cut on a stick on the account room door.

Winter Town on the fourth and fifth days, with the reeve and the works keeper. The market hall with its weekly market under it and the weighhouse beside it with the sealed set on its chain, worn bright at the pan. The two warehouses with Stark's bought grain in one and the independent weavers' bolts in the other for a weekly storage charge, which the reeve had thought of and Jon had not. The winter hall with eighty in it, the keeper's count chalked on the door jamb, and the infirmary with three beds filled and Lew's book open on the table in a hand that had got no worse. The brewer’s widow had both baths open. The privies, which did not stink, and the fire casks, full, and the low lane rebuilt in shingle with a cart's width more to it than before. The square's setts, with the relaid yard at the well corner showing paler than the rest. The lanes, which the reeve walked without looking down. The mill on the far bank of the race with its wheel turning in the winter water and the stocks falling on Orro's last bolts; the tannery below it, twenty pits under their lids and the journeyman standing over them with his arms folded as he had stood for two years. Six new houses by the mill and four in the lane where the chandler's had burned. The reeve said the town had five hundred and sixty households now by his count and had been five hundred when the roll was read, and that he could not say how many of the sixty had come for the hall and how many for the mill and how many because the lanes were dry.

The Acorn Water on the sixth, with the carpenter and Cerwyn's maester, who brought the south bank's tallies to set against the north's. The arch stood with ice on its parapets and the kingsroad crossing it, and the two tallies disagreed over a small charge, which the maester said was the lime, and the carpenter said was the maester. Lord Cerwyn rode down to meet him there and said that he had crossed it every week since the ninth moon of 127 and that he would miss the ford a little, as a man missed a bad tooth.

### The account

The examination opened in the great hall on the eighth day of the first moon with the doors open, as Jon had said, and every officer of the roll at the long table below the dais: the steward, the clerk and his two boys, the stores officer, the watch officer, the maester, the carpenter, the reeve, the works keeper, Orro, the journeyman tanner, the millwright's man, Hobb, Lew, the brewer's widow, Berrick for Lord Desmond, and a bench of tenants and townsmen who had come to see what an examination was. It ran five days. The steward read; the clerk kept the sheet; Jon asked; and what was asked and answered was written down beside the figure it concerned.

The steward read the total first: four hundred and seventy-seven thousand two hundred dragons paid. Nothing remained owed under the roll. He accounted separately for timber, wool and grain issued from Stark’s stores. The undrawn allowance was still in the vault. He said the accounts were not pretty in every column, then began with the ones that were.

The castle had six new granaries, three wells, the paved approach and working yards, standard measures, an account room and a school. The stores officer had never asked for the other four granaries. Nobody wanted the proposed baths while the castle had its springs, and the unused yards had caused no trouble.

The hall and market square had cost more than estimated; the lanes had cost less after the reeve measured them. The town’s trading money was partly tied up in yarn and bolts. The fifty-horse stable and three further baths had found no customers and no builder had been hired for them. The baths already standing burned wood. The hot water had never reached the town.

The estate receivers answered for the village wells, four granaries, a wool house, four bridges and the poles. The hide house had been cancelled when the stores officer found that the hides were sold with the beasts. Hornwood had better pine for tar than Stark’s woods. No receiver had named villages for most of the remaining wells. One digger had died. His crew’s new shoring rules were read with the account.

The clerk set each lord’s sworn sheet beside Winterfell’s tally, checked the seals and entered the materials and labour supplied against the agreement. In two cases the sheets disagreed; he noted the difference and moved to the next. The Acorn Water’s north bank, Locke’s shelving bank and Dustin’s causeway had needed extra work. Only Locke, Mormont and the Flint of Flint’s Finger had drawn commercial advances. The four clan stores were gifts.

The fairs had covered their costs and paid the Crown. The fish stations had returned a little of their advances. The cloth still lost money, and the leather receipts had barely begun to repay the yard. The bath leases were paid. The steward looked down the list of wells, stores, roads and public buildings and said nobody had promised him tolls from those.

Jon asked for the stores officer’s sheet. More grain was under sound roofs, though much of the increase was the reserve he had bought. No cart had been lost at the Acorn Water since the arch opened. The Hornwood landing handled more lighters, Winter Town had sixty more households, and four boys from the school now cut tallies for Stark. The clerk laid that sheet beside the charge and discharge.

### What was decided

He decided the rest on the fifth day with the sheet before him and the people who would do it at the table.

The cloth. The three years on one sheet said what he had thought: that Stark's looms lost a little coin every season on the book's prices and that the nine weavers who bought yarn at their own risk did not. He would not weave for a loss to prove a point. The six Stark looms were leased to the nine independent weavers at a rent of twenty stags a loom a year, the lord's wool sold to them at the spinners' cost plus a tenth as it had been, the mill fulling for anyone at a fee the millwright's man would set, and Orro paid for the remainder of his contract to teach them to finish, with the Braavosi contract passed to them as a body if the factor would take it, which Berrick said he would, since it was the same cloth from the same mill. The spinners in the villages kept their wheels and their wage.

The tannery. The journeyman had asked for twenty more pits and been refused and now said, before the hall, that he wanted none of it if the lord did not mean to make it a yard; and Jon let the shed, the pits and the wells to him at a rent of the bark's yearly value, with the hides sold to him at the book, and told him that if the yard paid his wage and the bark in two years he could dig what pits he liked on his own account. The journeyman said he would not take it and then asked what the bark had cost, and took it.

The buildings struck from the roll, by name, so that no later clerk would find them pending: four castle granaries, two castle baths, four acres of yards, the fifty-horse stable, three town baths, the hide house, the twenty tar kilns, two wool houses, six rural granaries, two bridges, twenty-four further wells, and every package offered to a lord who had not asked. The roll was closed. The unspent allowance remained in the vault.

The keeping page was read building by building, with the payer named beside each. The school, infirmary, winter hall, works keeper and repairs would need about two thousand six hundred and eighty dragons a year from the household. The mill would maintain itself from its fees; the lords would keep their own bridges. Jon made the household charge standing, like the Watch’s gift, and told the steward to enforce the upkeep clauses.

The second roll. The steward asked, on the fifth day, whether a second roll was to be written for 129, since Tallhart had asked for a third bridge and Glover for a third and the Wull for a second store and the receivers of three estates for wells and Lord Desmond's factor for a third warehouse floor, and since the first roll had shown what the works cost and what they did not pay. Jon said he would answer that after he had dealt with the succession question.

### The thirtieth day

The maester brought the letter on the twenty-eighth, more than a fortnight after the examination had ended. It was Lord Desmond's, in his own hand, written on the twentieth. The king had not left his bed since the autumn and was not expected to live out the winter, and the Hand's clerks had written to every lord in the realm with a copy of the king's old decree naming the princess his heir and a letter under it that did not mention her; and the princess's people on Dragonstone had written to every lord as well, under her seal, reminding them of the oath they had sworn to her in 105. Lord Desmond had both letters on his table and had answered neither and would answer as Winterfell answered. He asked, for the third time in three years and more plainly than before, what that would be.

On the evening of the thirtieth, Jon read it in the solar with the roll's closed account in its chest beside him and the horse roll under it and the stores officer's stick on the door. The steward was waiting for his word on the second roll. The maester was waiting for his word to White Harbor. Arra, who had sat in the hall for five days and said nothing, said from the window that her father would want the same word as Manderly and would want it from a man's mouth and not a raven, and that the track was open.
