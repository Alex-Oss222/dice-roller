## Turn 3 | Thirtieth day of the fourth moon, 126 AC | Evening

Elapsed: Forty-seven days, from the morning of the thirteenth day of the third moon to the evening of the thirtieth day of the fourth moon.

### The west tower

He went up the same morning, with the steward and nobody else. The guard on the stair was one of the south gate men he had put there in the twelfth moon, and he opened the door without being told to and shut it behind them.

Bennard had two rooms, the outer one with a table and the inner one with the bed, and a brazier, and a window with a bar across it that looked over the godswood wall. He had been given the books he asked for in the first moon and three of them were open on the table. He stood when Jon came in. He had lost weight since the trial and his beard had gone grey on one side.

“You've come,” he said. “I'd stopped expecting it.”

“You asked three times. What is it?”

“To see my son.”

“No.”

“Then to know he's alive. Nobody will tell me. The guard says he's not to speak of him and the maester says the same.”

Jon said the maester saw Benjen every week, and that from now on he would tell Bennard once a moon whether his son was well, and no more than that. Bennard asked for his wife's letters. Jon said Margaret could write and he could answer, and the maester would read both. Bennard said that was more than he had looked for, and sat down, and then said the thing he had sent for Jon to hear.

“You've had my rolls eight months. Did your clerk find the notes?”

“Which notes.”

“Then he didn't.” Bennard looked at the steward and back. “I lent money while I held the seal. The Dreadfort had one hundred and twenty thousand dragons from me in the year your father was two years dead, when Bolton's Braavosi were at his door. Barrowton had one hundred thousand the year after, when Dustin's horses had eaten his credit at White Harbor. Notes of hand, both, under their seals, repayable at the regent's pleasure. They're not in the rolls. I kept them in my own casket, the iron one with the two locks, which your men took with my goods and sealed, I'm told, and have not opened, because it was my goods and not the regency's.” He put his hands flat on the table. “I never spent a dragon that wasn't the regency's to spend. Those were mine to lend.”

“What do you want for them?”

“Benjen in the room above this one. I'd hear him walk.”

“No. The sentence is separate guard and it stays separate guard. He'll have a brazier of his own and the maester's word to you each moon. You'll tell me where the key is because it's Stark's coin you lent and you were Stark's regent when you lent it, and because if you don't, the clerk will have the casket open with a chisel by noon and your rooms apart by supper. The brazier is his either way.”

Bennard sat for a while with his hands where they were. Then he said the key was in the lining of the red book on the table, the one he had asked for by name in the first moon, and that Jon could have had it then if anyone had asked him what he wanted the book for.

The casket was in the sealed store under the maester's turret with the rest of his goods. The clerk opened it before Jon and the steward and wrote down what was in it: letters from Margaret, a lock of someone's hair, a purse of silver, and two folded sheets. The first was under the Dreadfort's seal and said one hundred and twenty thousand dragons, received of Bennard Stark, Regent, in the twelfth moon of 122, repayable at his pleasure. The second was under Dustin's and said one hundred thousand, in the fifth moon of 123, on the same terms. Both were witnessed by the maester of Winterfell. Jon asked him about it that evening. He said he had witnessed everything the regent sealed, as he had witnessed everything Lord Rickon sealed before him, and that the notes had gone into the regent's own casket and not the rolls, and that he had not thought of them from that day to this. Jon had that written down too.

### Donnel

The sergeant came down on the eighth day of the fourth moon with five men, two Norrey garrons and no packs. He reported in the solar standing, because he had not sat on anything but a saddle for four days and said he would rather not start.

The track was open as far as the stone and bad past it. The first bad place was as the Flint's men had said. At the second, the ledge, the lead pack beast had put a hind foot over the edge where the shale was loose and gone down forty feet with one bag of salt, and they had got the other bag off it with a rope and left the carcass for the Flint's people. Nobody was hurt. Arra had walked the ledge ahead of the garrons and had them led over one at a time with a man at the head and a man at the tail. They had reached the Norrey's hall on the fifth day at dusk.

“He fed us for three days, my lord, and wouldn't let me go until the third morning. His words, as near as I have them.” Donnel had them by heart and gave them without looking at anything. “Tell the Stark the poles yes, and the axes yes, and the iron. Tell him the store he can build where my hand goes, with my key in it and his grain, and I'll come down when the lambs are off the ewes and drink his mead and look at his timber before I say more. Tell him the Wull is coming with me and the Liddle if his knee lets him. And tell him I never thought a Stark would wed a Norrey, and I'm not a fool, and my daughter is where she wants to be.”

“And the Flint?”

“Poles, my lord. He'll cut them himself and wants the axes sent up with the Norrey's. The Knott sent back the letter unopened. The Burley and the Harclay hadn't answered when I left.”

Jon asked how the hall was. Donnel said it was a long hall of stone and turf with the beasts in one end and the people in the other, and that it was warmer than the south gate, and that the Norrey had forty men who could have cut the escort to pieces at the ledge if he had wanted to, and had sent four of them down with Donnel as far as the stone to carry the salt that was left. The lost bag was written off at the book price. Jon gave the sergeant and his six twenty silver stags each from his own purse and the garrons went back to the sheepwalks.

### The thaw

The ground went soft in the last week of the third moon, and after that the works that had waited on it went forward together, which meant that the carpenter was wanted in four places at once and the steward spent his days deciding which.

The well in the grain yard went down first. The digger and his two men took it four feet a day through clay, shoring with planks as they went, and struck water at twenty-two feet on the ninth day. They dug three feet into it, laid the stone lining up from the bottom on a timber curb, and built the kerb and windlass above. The water was tested by letting it stand in a jar for a day and then drinking it, which the digger did himself. The well in the stable court went down fifteen feet and came in brown, and the digger put his nose to the bucket and said it was the dung heap on the other side of the wall and had been for a hundred years, and that no lining would keep it out. Jon had it filled and the site moved outside the court to the lane behind the kennels, abandoning the work already paid for. The third well, behind the smithy, was begun in the last week of the fourth moon.

The two granaries went up on their pads. The carpenter set the sills on stone piers two feet clear of the ground, so that the floors stood on air and the rats would have to climb; the frames were pegged in the woodyard and carried over in pieces and raised with sheer-legs; by the end of the fourth moon both frames stood and the first had its rafters. The roof of the old north granary was stripped the same week and the rot in the wall plate was found to go further than the patch, which was the kind of thing that was always found once a roof was off.

The paving stone stopped coming when the fields softened, at eleven hundred and eighty loads. The paviours were a master from White Harbor, come up with the factors, and five men he hired in Winter Town, and they began the approach from the gate outward: the old surface lifted, the gravel bed laid and rammed, the sand spread two inches deep, the rubble set on edge and rammed to the line. They made twelve square yards a man a day, as the roll had guessed, and the master said the approach would be done by the end of the sixth moon if the stone held out, which it would not, and Jon told the steward to find more while the wagons could still go to the pit by the road.

In Winter Town the tenders came in. The carpenter and a mason from the town priced the market hall above the roll’s allowance. Jon accepted after the carpenter showed him the heavier roof timbers it required. The weighhouse, the two warehouses and the winter hall were taken at prices near the roll's. The stable for fifty horses was not let; the reeve said no fair day had ever brought fifty horses that needed a roof, and Jon held it. The infirmary house and the school house were let together at the book price for a plain house each. The privies were dug and lined through the fourth moon and stank less than the lanes they replaced. Two of the old wells were relined and two of the new six dug; the fire casks came from the cooper and were set, filled and counted by the watch, which had never counted anything in Winter Town before and said so.

The bath was the one thing that failed before it was built. The carpenter dug his trench from the east wall outfall toward the town and found by the third day what a level would have told him sooner: the town stood six feet above the ditch where the castle's warm water ran out, and no pipe would carry water uphill. The first bath would heat its own water with wood. Jon had it sited at the east end of the main street, clear of the privies, and told the steward to find a keeper who would lease it before a tub was bought.

Lord Desmond's factors came up on the twenty-second. The senior one was a Manderly man named Berrick who had sold wool at Braavos for twenty years and looked at the castle's fleeces the way the Norrey girl looked at a horse. He said northern fleece was coarse and short in the staple and would make a grey cloth that sold at the low price and sold steadily, and that nobody in Braavos would mistake it for broadcloth whatever the roll called it. He would take two thousand yards of the first season's cloth at an agreed price per yard if it were properly fulled and he could see the fulling. He had no weaving master to give and said the Braavosi house would answer in its own time. Six looms were ordered from a Winter Town joiner. There were nine weavers in the town already, in their own houses, and the steward's first task was to find out whether any of them wanted to weave for Stark's wool at Stark's wage, which not all of them did.

The receivers' returns came in from the twelve estates with the villages that lacked water named, thirteen of them, and a note from the Upper White Knife receiver that his village did not lack water, it lacked a well that did not fall in, which the clerk entered as the same thing.

### The instalment

The tribute came in over two weeks from the tenth of the fourth moon, as it had come in every year, by wagon under each lord's own escort, with each lord's steward or his clerk riding with it and a sealed tally in his coat.

The counting was done in the vault on the long table under three keys: Jon's, the steward's and the clerk's. Each sack was opened before the lord's man, the coin was counted onto the table in hundreds and the hundreds into stacks of ten, and each thousand was weighed on the vault balance against the standard weight before it went back into a sack under Winterfell's seal. The lord's man and the clerk each cut the count into a tally stick and split it, and the lord's man took his half away with the receipt. Where the count fell short of the roll, the shortfall was written against the house with the man's own explanation beside it, and where coin came in that was not current, Dornish or old Braavosi or clipped, it was weighed, not counted, and entered at its weight.

Bolton’s steward brought the Dreadfort’s share in full and its old arrears in a separate sack, as his lord had written, and said nothing about anything else. Umber’s current share came in full; his old arrears did not. Mormont’s and the two Flints’ payments had not arrived, and Locke’s and Ryswell’s came short. Their men spoke of the roads and the price of wool. Ryswell’s man offered no explanation. On the twenty-seventh the Crown’s share went down the kingsroad in four chests under thirty men, with Lord Desmond’s undertaking to ship it south and a sealed letter to the king’s treasurer.

Jon settled the Umber claim on the twenty-sixth. The clerk had found nine thousand quarters sent to the Wall at the regent’s order and never allowed. Jon struck sixteen thousand dragons from the arrears for grain and carriage, leaving thirty-two thousand owed. He enclosed the clerk’s sheet and asked Umber to send any writing that promised him a higher price. The letter went by raven. The notes from Bennard’s casket went into the works chest under Jon’s seal; nothing was written to the Dreadfort or Barrowton about them yet.

### Court

He held the gate court on the fifth day of each week and one case from the fourth moon mattered more than the rest. A shepherd's boy from the northern sheepwalks, sixteen, had driven eleven of the lord's ewes off the walk in the second moon and sold them at the fair at a low price to a Winter Town butcher who had not asked about the mark. The reeve's tally had caught it, because eleven ewes with the Stark notch had been sold by a boy who owned no sheep. The boy said the ewes were strays. The butcher said he had bought what was offered. Jon had the boy's father brought in, who had a holding of nine acres on the walk and had not known, and judged it this way: the price of eleven ewes at the book rate charged against the father's holding and taken in wool at the shearing; the boy to work a year on the road gang at the gang’s ordinary pay and board, sleeping where the gang slept; the butcher fined the price of the ewes a second time for buying marked stock without the mark, and told that the next time he would lose his stall. The reeve had expected a hand taken and said so afterward, and Jon said a hand on the walk was no use to anyone and a year on the road was.

Cerwyn's maester sent his sworn survey on the twenty-sixth. The castle-road ford was twenty-six feet bank to bank at summer water with gravel under it and would take the book's thirty-foot span. The Acorn Water crossing on the kingsroad was forty-eight feet, with a soft bank on the north side, and would not. The maester wrote that a timber bridge there needed a pier in the river and two spans, and that he could not price it and would not guess. Jon wrote back that he would come and look at it himself when the water was down in the summer and that nothing was to be cut for it until he had.

On the evening of the thirtieth the clerk closed the instalment account, with the Crown’s share sent and the unpaid sums carried forward. The steward read what waited on Jon: the Norrey’s coming after the lambing, the Acorn Water, the measures, a keeper for the bath, and the letters to the Dreadfort and Barrowton.
