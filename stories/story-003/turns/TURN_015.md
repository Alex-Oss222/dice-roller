## Turn 15 | 129/04/05 | Evening
Elapsed: Thirty-four days.

### At the Moat

The direwolf went up over each standing tower and beside the road into the encampment. Smaller standards marked the company streets. From the northern approach a traveller could see whose army was gathering before he reached the first picket.

Jon had the captains bring forward men who knew a trade. The carpenter asked each what work he had actually done. A man who had helped raise a barn went with the timber gang, not among the masons. Wheelwrights, smiths, sawyers and coopers were entered separately as the companies arrived.

The mornings were for drill. Men practised keeping their intervals, changing front and withdrawing without turning a retreat into a race. Jon worked along the ranks with a blunted spear, correcting a thrust or taking a captain's place for an exchange. In the late afternoons and evenings the work gangs repaired flooring, roofing, drains and the worst stretches of the approach. Guards, cooks and wagon teams kept their duties; the same men were not required to stand the night watch after a full day's heavy work.

At the stores shed they lifted the lower boards, cut out two rotten bearers and bedded replacements on flat stones. The carpenters bored the joints and drove oak pins through them. Labourers carried the spoiled timber out while the roofers stripped the broken shingles above. The sacks went back only after the floor was swept.

The leaning tower's upper masonry remained beyond the work they could safely do with the present timber and lifting gear. The camp gained dry stores, repaired doors and firmer walking surfaces, not another sound castle tower.

Jon took his meals with different companies and heard the men after drill. One spearman insisted he was fit for everything his captain asked.

“Nothing wrong with my feet.”

Both heels were blistered open beneath the boot seams. Jon had him wash them, saw them dressed, and put him on seated work until he could wear his boots without opening them again. Other complaints were less visible: a family's missing letter, a damaged cooking pot, oats promised to a wagon team but issued to another company. The appropriate officer had to answer the man, not merely take his name.

Each day Jon received a different lord where one was present, or the captain answering for an absent lord. Cerwyn came first, then Tallhart; Dustin, Ryswell, Hornwood and Glover followed as their companies reached the Moat. Not every lord had left his own lands.

Roderick Dustin wanted to know whether a southern commander could take his men away in small detachments.

“No,” Jon said. “Our men remain under a commander appointed by Winterfell.”

“Then tell the captains when there is an agreement. They have heard three different accounts of what we are doing here.”

Jon went through the standing orders with them. They were training and provisioning at the Moat. They had no order to cross it as an army.

The supply wagons continued coming from the houses. Grain was measured into the camp stores, meat and fish issued to the cooks, and fodder kept on its own account. Arra sent a field chest under guard; the clerk paid the companies' agreed camp charges against their returns. The cooks reported what remained after the last men in line had eaten.

### Riders and letters

Six men went south in three pairs. They were chosen from riders who had carried goods or worked the river roads before, rather than men whose only preparation was taking off a badge. They travelled as carriers, traders and ordinary travellers through the Neck towards the Green Fork and the roads leading farther into the riverlands. They were to bring back what they saw and distinguish it from what someone had told them.

The first pair returned on the twenty-fifth. They had found new guards at crossings and seen Frey men collecting provisions. They had not seen a great host marching north. One had heard that Harrenhal had fallen; neither had been there. The other four remained out, with their reports to follow by the arranged carriers.

By then two proclamations had reached Jon through Winterfell. Aegon's arrived on the nineteenth; Rhaenyra's four days later. Viserys was dead. Both his son and his daughter had been crowned. A later White Harbor report placed Daemon and his dragon at Harrenhal. The copies went into the travelling papers without a northern oath being sent in answer.

Jon's first letter to Arra gave her the Watch negotiations outright.

“The matter is yours. Negotiate and settle it as you judge best. You need not send each clause to me for approval. I trust your judgment.”

Their letters passed with riders on the supply road. She reported the household business, asked after the camp and told him which accounts Hobb was teaching her to examine. Her first report from the Wall said that the Lord Commander would not surrender either Gift. She continued the talks without asking Jon to change his instructions.

Her completed settlement reached him on the thirtieth.

“They will not give us ownership,” she wrote. “Their answer on that did not change. Their steward did, however, name six deserted villages in the New Gift for which they receive almost nothing. I asked about those.”

With the maester checking the wording and Hobb working through the account, she had agreed a five-year arrangement for those six villages and their defined lands. Winterfell could bring in willing settlers, appoint reeves and administer the holdings. The Watch retained ownership and its reserved jurisdiction. Six tenths of the rents and customary land dues actually collected would go to the Watch, in coin or kind; four tenths would go to Winterfell. Stark's rebuilding and collection costs came out of its own share, not by reducing the Watch's six tenths first.

The inhabitants would not become a source of levies for the southern war. Accounts would be examined together each year. Arra had reserved twenty-four thousand dragons for survey, seed and initial repairs, to be released against the work rather than paid away with the signatures. No settlers had yet moved and no new rent had been collected.

“I have signed. It is less land than you wanted, and it is not ours. I accepted it rather than pay men to argue about the whole Gift for another year. The survey and the settlers are my next work.”

The agreement required no further approval from Jon. Neither Gift had been ceded; the limited administration and revenue arrangement was nevertheless concluded, and Arra retained charge of carrying it out.

### The prince

By the fifth day of the fourth moon, seven thousand two hundred men stood on the expedition roll. Four were still scouting south of the Neck and eighteen were excused drill with illness or injuries. The remainder were in camp and fit for duty. The greater part of every contributing house's strength remained at home.

Seven hundred and forty quarters remained in the companies' grain stores. The next deliveries were already being arranged. The larger muster had cost money, but the men were being fed, their service paid and the existing defenses kept manned.

Word of Jacaerys came ahead of him from White Harbor. Manderly had told the prince where Jon was; the rider carrying his notice reached the Moat before the dragon.

Vermax landed beyond the busiest part of the camp. The pickets took the horses farther back while the prince unfastened his riding harness. A captain met him on foot and brought him through the company streets to Jon's quarters.

Jacaerys was brown-haired and younger than most of the men watching him pass. He carried his mother's letter himself.

“Lord Manderly said I would find you here, Lord Stark. My mother asks the North to uphold its oath.” He laid the sealed letter on the table. “I have come to hear your answer.”

Arra independently concluded the five-year, six-village New Gift agreement and reserved 24,000 GD for its implementation. The Watch retains title; outright recovery of both Gifts was refused. She continues the work under delegated authority, without a decision being returned to Jon.
