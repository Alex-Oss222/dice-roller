## Turn 7 | Thirtieth day of the sixth moon, 127 AC | Evening

Elapsed: A hundred and twenty days, from the evening of the thirtieth day of the second moon to the evening of the thirtieth day of the sixth moon.

### The spring

The voussoirs came up from White Harbor on the first carts after the thaw, forty-one dressed stones to the cart and eleven carts, each stone numbered on its bed with the mason's chisel, and Master Wyl behind them on a mule. He had the south abutment up on the gravel by the end of the fourth moon, in coursed hill stone with a lime core, and the centering frames made on the south bank from Cerwyn's seasoned timber: four trusses the shape of the arch's underside, to be stood on the abutments when the river fell and the voussoirs laid over them from both springers until they met. He would not stand them while the river was in flood and said so to everyone who asked, which was most of Cerwyn's tenants.

Jon rode north on the eighth day of the fourth moon with twelve men, the carpenter having gone ahead to the Last Hearth a week before. Before he went he did a thing he had not done before and gave his key of the vault to the maester, with a sheet in his own hand saying that the maester held it for the counting of the fourth-moon instalment and the sending of the Crown's share and for nothing else, and that the steward and the clerk were to count as if Jon stood at the table. The steward said it was the first time in his service that an instalment had been counted without the lord's own key in his hand. Jon said that if the three of them could not count coin without him the North had worse troubles than a debt at the Dreadfort.

### The Dreadfort

The Dreadfort stood on its hill above the Weeping Water with the flayed man on every tower, six days' ride east through country that got poorer as the road got worse. The Lord of the Dreadfort met him in the yard, a lean man of fifty with a quiet voice who had never raised it in Jon's hearing and, by Cregan's memory, had never needed to. He gave Jon the best rooms and the best supper and the first thirty thousand dragons of the note on the second morning, in his own hall, counted by his steward onto his own table before Jon and Jon's clerk, in silver stags mostly and some gold, with the count cut into two sticks. He said nothing of what the money had been for in 122 and Jon did not ask him.

He had done a thing Jon had not expected. His steward had been at the Winter Town fair in the second moon and had walked through the two new granaries behind the castle with the stores officer, who showed anyone who asked, and had come home and told his lord, and the lord had built two of the same at the Dreadfort over the winter from his own timber at his own charge, with the floors on piers and the eaves wide, and had told Winterfell nothing about it. He showed Jon them on the second afternoon without any particular pride, as he might have shown him a new well, and said the one thing he had not been able to copy was the stores officer, and asked whether the boys in Winterfell's school would be for hire when they could count. Jon said they would be Stark's clerks first. The lord said that was what he had supposed.

He named his four villages for the wells, at last, and Jon had the clerk write the agreement there, Stark's six parts and the Dreadfort's four in labor, with the lord's seal on it before supper.

On the last night, with the hall emptied and the two of them and the maesters at the high table, the Lord of the Dreadfort asked what Winterfell would do if the king died and the succession were disputed, which he said everyone from here to the Wall was asking and nobody from here to the Wall would ask Lord Stark to his face. Jon said that Winterfell would keep the North's oath and the North's peace, in that order, and that the Dreadfort would hear what that meant when it meant something. The lord said that was an answer he would have given himself. They parted with every courtesy and nothing settled, which Jon had expected, and which was the Dreadfort's way of being on good terms.

### The Last Hearth

The Last Hearth was seven days further north, through the Lonely Hills and across the Last River, and Lord Umber met him at the ford on a horse the size of a plough ox, roaring.

The roaring was about the roof. He had the four granaries lined up below the hall on the river flat, three of them sound and one of them with its ridge down a foot and its gable split and its spoiled barley heaped outside it under a sail, and he had his carpenter there and Winterfell's carpenter there and he wanted it said in front of both of them whose fault it was.

Winterfell's carpenter said it in front of both of them. He had been up on the roof two days. The frames were Winterfell's timber and were sound; he had cut into a post and showed the heart. The purlins were not Winterfell's timber; they were the Last Hearth's own pine, cut that autumn and green, and they had bowed under the first snow and let the ridge down. And the pitch was the Last Hearth's pitch, which was flat because Umber roofs had always been flat, because they were turf, and a plank roof under four feet of snow was not a turf roof. Umber's carpenter said the lord had told him to use his own purlins because Winterfell's were too short. Lord Umber said he had said nothing of the kind, and then said that if he had, the carpenter should have said they were green, and then said nothing for a while.

Jon settled it standing in front of the split gable. Winterfell would send timber for one new roof, purlins and all, at the pitch Winterfell's carpenter drew on the ground with a stick, as a gift and not as a charge on the roll; the Last Hearth would re-pitch the other three at its own cost before the next snow or lose grain again, which was its own affair; and the thirty-two thousand dragons were owed and were due. Umber looked at the stick drawing and at the split gable and at Jon, and then laughed in a way that made the horses move, and said that the Stark had come three hundred miles to be told he was wrong about a roof and had paid for the privilege, and that the thirty-two thousand would be on the table at supper.

It was, in silver and in old gold with Aegon's head on it, counted by Umber's clerk and Jon's into two sticks, and Umber drank to it.

The next day was his sons' day. The second son, a man of twenty-two with his father's shoulders and six inches on Jon, asked in the yard after breakfast whether the Lord of Winterfell would try a bout with blunts, as Lord Cregan had done at Winterfell in his father's time, and his father said the boy meant no harm by it, and Jon said he knew what the boy meant by it and took the sword.

They fought on the hard earth of the yard with blunted longswords and no armor but padded jacks, which was the Last Hearth's custom. The son had the reach and the strength and came on with them, long cuts from the high guard that would have split a plank, and Jon gave ground in a half circle to keep the sun out of his own eyes and in the son's, and let three of the cuts go by close enough to feel the wind of them, and did not strike at all. The fourth cut came the same way as the third, because the son had found that nothing happened when he cut, and Jon stepped inside it before it had fallen, bound the blade down with his own and put his left hand on the son's right elbow and his hip into him, and the son went sideways onto one knee with his sword point in the dirt. He was up at once, faster than Jon had allowed for, and the backhand he threw from the ground caught Jon across the left shoulder hard enough to be felt a week later. Jon took it, closed again while the son's arm was still out, and put the point of the blunt against the padded jack over the heart and held it there until Umber shouted.

Umber said his son had hit the Stark. His son said the Stark had let him, and Jon said he had not, and that was the truth and the son knew it and was pleased with it for the rest of the visit, which was the point of fighting a bout at the Last Hearth. The shoulder bruised black and Jon rode south with his arm stiff for six days and said nothing of it to anyone who had not seen it.

### Karhold

Karhold was five days south along the coast road, a grey keep on a headland with the sea on three sides, and Lord Karstark received him with the correctness of a man who had married a daughter of his house to a convicted traitor and had the lady in his hall. Margaret Karstark sat at supper two places down from Jon and asked after her husband and her son in a steady voice before the whole table, because, as she said, there was nobody there who did not know. Jon told her what the maester told Bennard each month, that Benjen had been sick in the winter and was well, and that Bennard read and was fed and had her letters. She asked whether she might come to Winterfell and see them. He said she might, in the tenth moon when the roads were dry, for a week, in the maester's company, and she thanked him and ate her supper and did not speak of it again.

The spinning house was forty women in a long room above the cattle byre, warm from the beasts below, spinning the Karhold fleece on wheels that Karstark's own wheelwright had made from a drawing. The yarn went to Winter Town by cart at the fairs and the hall markets and sold as fast as it came. Lord Karstark asked for nothing more. The two granaries were on their foundations on the river flat below the keep and would be roofed before harvest, by a carpenter who had been shown the pitch.

### The Hornwood

He came back by the Hornwood, four days west and south through the forest, and Lord Hornwood walked him through the six kilns on the north side where the old pine stood: clay-lined pits with a channel at the bottom and a barrel under the channel, each with its burner, the forest around them smelling of tar for a mile. Two hundred gallons stood in barrels at the landing on the White Knife waiting for Berrick's lighters, and Hornwood had the first coin in a chest and wanted a wharf at the landing, because the lighters grounded on the mud at every ebb and the tar and the grain waited for the water.

The landing was the one place on the White Knife above White Harbor where a lighter could lie against the bank, and Stark's grain had gone down from it for fifty years. Jon walked the bank with Hornwood and his reeve, had the depth taken at the ebb with a pole, and agreed a sixty-foot timber wharf there as a public work from the roll's inventory, Stark's six parts and the Hornwood's four in timber and labor, with Master Wyl to price it when the arch was turned and no sooner. Hornwood said that was the first thing Winterfell had ever built on his land and he supposed he would have to let his grandchildren pay for the upkeep. Jon said the agreement said the Hornwood kept it sound, and Hornwood said he had read the agreement.

They hunted red deer in the Hornwood on the last day, with the lord's hounds, and killed two, and Jon's shoulder let him draw a bow and he did not say what it cost him.

### Winterfell

He was home on the twenty-second day of the fifth moon, forty-four days gone. The instalment had been counted without him. Collections were better than the year before; Ryswell had paid his shortfall, Mormont’s money had arrived on time by ship, and the Crown’s share had gone south with its portion of the fair. The maester returned Jon’s key and said the three of them had counted everything twice. Bolton’s payment had come with the tribute wagons in its own sack. Umber’s had travelled in Jon’s saddlebags.

The thirty measure sets had come from White Harbor with the spring grain carts, each with the six merchants' marks on its receipt, and the stores officer's deputy with them, who said the White Harbor scale-smiths were better than the castle smith and the castle smith should not be told. Nineteen sets went out to the lords who had asked, with a receipt for each naming its custody, and sixteen stayed in the chest.

The side lanes had been measured again by the reeve, fourteen thousand square yards and not the roll's seventeen, and let to the master paviour with a new foreman at the new measured price, and begun in the fifth moon from the square outward. By the end of the sixth moon a quarter of them were laid, and then the hay was ready and every man who could swing a scythe went to it, as Jon had told the paviour they would, and the lanes stopped for a month.

The four rural granaries went up through the fifth and sixth moons on their piers. The east meadow site, which the receiver had chosen on the flat by the village for the carts' sake, had flooded in the spring thaw to a foot deep, and the frame was taken down before it was finished and carried to the rise above the village, earning the receiver a letter he would keep. The northern sheepwalk wool house was framed by the shearing and took the second season's fleeces under a roof. The tannery had its two wells and its first forty hides in the pits under lime, and the journeyman said they would be in the pits until the year after next and that was that. The millwright's men dug the wheel pit on the far bank of the race through the fifth and sixth moons and the sluice frame went in.

Master Wyl stood his centering on the twelfth day of the sixth moon, when the Acorn Water had dropped to the gravel, four trusses on the two abutments with their feet on wedges so that they could be struck when the arch was closed, and his men began to lay the voussoirs on them from both springers at once, each stone to its number, bedded in lime. Jon rode down to see it on the fifteenth and stood under the centering with the mason while the stones went up above their heads, and asked how long. Master Wyl said six weeks to the keystone if the river stayed down and his men stayed sober, and that he would answer for the river sooner than the men.

Lord Desmond's letter came in the sixth moon with the lighter count from the Hornwood landing, which was up by nine on the year before, and a long page of news from the south that he said was no longer gossip. The king had held a great feast at King's Landing in the first moon of the year to make peace between his queen's children and his daughter's, and the peace had lasted as far as the pudding, and there had been a brawl between the princes and blood drawn, and the princess had gone back to Dragonstone with her sons and the queen's party held the court. The king was mending from a winter's sickness, slowly. Lord Desmond said that White Harbor's ships were asked by both sides about their cargoes now and that he answered both the same. Jon read it twice and put it in the chest with the notes of hand, and told the steward that the lords' accounts of men and arms, which were due at harvest under the standing order, were to be exact this year and were to say which men had ridden to a muster in the last ten years and which had not.

At the end of the sixth moon the arch’s remainder and the lanes were the largest unpaid contracts. The steward asked about the brewer’s widow’s second bath. She had asked a third time and brought her takings to show him. Jon read them and agreed to the same lease: the roll would pay for the house and copper, and nothing after.
