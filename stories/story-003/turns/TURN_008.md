## Turn 8 | Thirtieth day of the tenth moon, 127 AC | Evening

Elapsed: A hundred and twenty days, from the evening of the thirtieth day of the sixth moon to the evening of the thirtieth day of the tenth moon.

### The keystone

Master Wyl set the keystone on the second day of the eighth moon, with the river low and the two half-arches grown toward each other over the centering until a single wedge of grey stone the width of a man's chest was all that stood between them. He had it lowered on the sheer-legs with his own hands on the ropes, bedded it in lime, and drove it the last inch with a timber maul, and then sat down on the parapet course and said that it was an arch now and not a pile of stones, and that nobody was to touch the wedges under the centering for four weeks whatever the river did.

The river did what the river did. The rains began on the tenth and did not stop for a fortnight, and the Acorn Water came up out of its gravel on the eighteenth and went on coming up until it stood against the alders at the old flood marks, with the centering's feet on their wedges two feet above it and then one foot and then less. Master Wyl did not sleep for two nights. Cerwyn's tenants, who had been told the bridge would fall and had come to see it, were put to work instead piling hill stone against the upstream struts of the centering while the mason stood in the water to his waist and told them where. The south abutment took the river on its gravel and did not move. The north abutment, on its raft over the coffer, did not move either. The water reached the wedges on the night of the twenty-first, touched them, and fell away on the morning of the twenty-second, and the arch stood on its centering with its lime still green and had not shifted by the width of a chisel.

Cerwyn's castle-road bridge, a mile up the other river, lost its north ramp in the same flood: the earth approach the road gang had thrown up from the cut washed out to the first pile and a hay cart went in to the axle on the twenty-fourth where the ramp had been. The piles and deck stood. Cerwyn had the ramp rebuilt in stone-faced earth by his own men at his own charge, as the agreement said he would, and wrote to Jon that he now understood the clause about upkeep in a way he had not when he sealed it.

The centering was struck on the sixth day of the ninth moon, the wedges knocked out one by one from the downstream side while the mason listened to the arch with his hand on the intrados, and the trusses let down and dragged out, and the arch stood on its own. Jon rode down for it. Lord Cerwyn, who had said he would believe in a stone bridge on the Acorn Water when he drove over it, drove over it on the eighth in his own cart, turned round on the kingsroad, and drove back, and said nothing at all. The parapets and the stone-faced approaches were finished through the ninth moon. The road gang, which had pulled eleven carts out of the ford in 126, pulled none out in the tenth moon of 127 because there was no longer a ford to pull them out of, and the foreman asked where the gang was to go next.

### The well

A digger was killed on the twelfth day of the ninth moon at the kingsroad north village, in the fourth of the six further wells Jon had ordered that year from the receivers' returns. The well was at eighteen feet in wet clay after the rains. The crew master had told his men to pull the shoring planks from the upper part of the shaft to use lower down, to save buying more, and the sides had come in above the digger while he was at the bottom and buried him to the chest, and he was dead before they had him out.

Jon rode to the village the next day with the carpenter and held the inquiry at the well head with the crew, the receiver and the dead man's widow present. The men said what the master had told them. The master said the clay had been standing well and the planks were wanted below. The carpenter put his hand in the wet clay of the shaft wall and it came away in a lump, and said that was the answer to that. Jon dismissed the crew master from Stark's works, fined him the price of the planks he had saved and the price of the digger's burial, and entered both against him. The widow had one hundred dragons from Jon's own purse and her holding's rent remitted for a year. The well was finished by the first crew with the shoring in and a man at the top, and gave water at twenty-one feet, and the village drank from it. The rule went into the works book and was read to every crew: below ten feet the planks stayed in until the lining was up, and a man stood at the top at all times who did nothing else, on the roll's pay. The steward said it would add forty dragons to every deep well. Jon said it would.

### West

He rode west on the sixteenth day of the ninth moon with ten men, Torrhen's Square and the Rills and Barrowton and home, three weeks, because the roads were made now as far as the Barrowton road went and he wanted to see what three years of coin had bought in the west before the tenth moon's accounts told him.

Master Tallhart had his second bridge half built, at the ford on the road to the Rills where his tenants had asked for it, and he had done a thing of his own: two wells dug at his own charge in two villages that the roll had not named, by a crew he had hired from Winterfell's digger when the roll's four were done, because, he said, he had watched the four being dug and it had not looked hard. One had water and one was dry. He had the dry one filled and said that was his schooling and he had paid for it.

Lord Ryswell at the Rills had nothing to show and did not pretend to. The murrain of 126 had taken a third of his horses and the wet summer had taken the hay, and the cloth works the roll had drawn for him had never had a stream, a fuller or a master and never would. He asked for two public wells for two villages that had carried water half a mile since his grandfather's time, and for nothing from the commercial purse, and Jon granted the wells on the spot and had them dug before the frost. Ryswell asked, at supper, whether a lord who had paid his tribute short two years running and had taken his overlord's coin for wells would be remembered for the one or the other. Jon said that depended on what he did in the third year.

Lord Dustin met him on his own road, on the twelfth mile, where the gravel ended and his own three miles beyond it began. He had finished the roll's twelve before the rains, with a raised causeway of stone and faggots across the bad mile at the ford that Jon walked and approved despite the extra cost. Beyond the twelfth mile Dustin's own tenants were laying gravel toward the Barrowton cattle market at Dustin's own charge, on the same section, with the same ditches, under the same reeve, because, Dustin said, a road that stopped three miles short of the market was a road to nowhere and he was not a man who built roads to nowhere. He had built no granary and said he had no use for one; the Barrowlands grew cattle, and cattle kept themselves.

He was sixty, grey, thick through the chest, with a voice that had carried over battles Cregan's father had told stories about, and he rode the twelve miles beside Jon at a walk and talked about horses, and about the twenty more he would send against the note at the instalment, and about nothing else until the last mile. Then he said that every lord in the North had the same letter from White Harbor about the feast and the princes, and that he was too old to care which Targaryen sat on which chair, but that if it came to a fight the North fought for whoever Winterfell said, as it always had, and that he would like to know in time to get his boots on. Jon said he would know in time. Dustin said that was all he wanted, and that Barrowton's hospitality was not the Last Hearth's but the beef was better, which it was.

### Harvest, and the tenth moon

The harvest came in wet. The rye and the oats had stood in the rain for a fortnight before the carts could get into the fields, and the grain that came up the approach was heavy with damp and had to be spread on the floors and turned, not heaped. The new granaries took it best: the raised floors and the open eaves drew the air under and through, and the stores officer had the heaps turned daily by the school's boys, who could count and could be spared. The old granaries, with their floors on the ground, heated in the bottom layer within the fortnight, and three hundred quarters went to the pigs from the north granary before the heap could be turned. The four rural granaries took their villages' grain for the first time and the receivers wrote that nothing had been carted to Winterfell from those four villages that year, which was the first time the receivers had written that.

The count on the twentieth day of the tenth moon was sixty-nine thousand eight hundred quarters in store, against seventy-four thousand one hundred the year before, a middling harvest and a wet one, with the loss counted this year because the grain had been weighed in: eleven hundred quarters between the doors and the count, three hundred of them to the pigs and the rest to damp and the turning. The stores officer said that was the first honest figure for loss that Winterfell had ever had and that it would be lower next year because he now knew where it was.

The second instalment was counted under three keys with Jon at the table. A dozen houses paid short after the wet year. The Crown’s share went south on the twenty-seventh. Dustin’s steward brought twenty-five thousand dragons and twenty horses against the regency note.

Margaret Karstark came on the twelfth and stayed a week, in the maester's company, as agreed. She saw Bennard twice and Benjen twice, with the maester in the room and the guard at the door, and the maester told Jon afterward only that she had wept with the one and not with the other. On the last evening she asked Jon, in the solar with the maester present, whether Benjen might be allowed to take the black, since the Wall took worse men than her son and he was twenty-six and would be sixty in that tower. Jon said the sentence was lifelong imprisonment under separate guard and that it stood, and that he had spared the two of them the axe and would not be asked for more. She said she had known the answer and had wanted to have asked. She went home by the coast road with the maester's letter to Karhold and nothing else.

The lords' accounts of men came in with the harvest, exact for the first time because the standing order had said exact. The clerk made them into one sheet. By their own counts, thirty-one thousand four hundred men in the North were fit to ride or march at a moon's notice; four thousand seven hundred of those had ridden to a muster in the last ten years; two thousand one hundred had horse and mail of their own. Jon read the sheet and had it locked in the chest with the notes and Lord Desmond's letters, and said to nobody that it was the lords' counting and not his.

The rest of the works went on as the summer allowed. The side lanes started again after the hay and were seven parts in ten laid by the frost, with the master paviour's new foreman's tally and the gate's agreeing to the load. The mill had its wheel hung and its stocks framed and would full cloth in the spring if the millwright's eighteen moons held. The tannery took forty more hides and its journeyman said the first forty would come out of the pits in the second moon of 128 and not before. Orro's second season gave nine thousand four hundred yards by the end of the tenth moon, finished under his eye, and Berrick's man passed seven thousand eight hundred of them at a better price than the year before and rejected the rest for the fulling, which the mill was to cure. The brewer's widow opened her second bath in the ninth moon. The wool house at the northern sheepwalk took the shearing under its roof. Glover's bridge went in after harvest and his road was done; Locke's wharf was finished at the end of the ninth moon, a month late, with an extra charge for the shelving bank; Mormont's granary was roofed; Cerwyn's and Karstark's granaries were roofed with the pitch shown; Bolton dug two of his four wells; the Hornwood's wharf was priced by Master Wyl at the book's price and a little, and its piles went in at the autumn ebbs; the Harclay's store stood at his valley mouth. Two of the roll's six bridges on Stark's own land went in after harvest at the mill-and-bridge villages' ford and the Upper White Knife crossing, and the road gang went to them.

At the end of the tenth moon the mason had been paid apart from his retention and had gone home to White Harbor. The steward asked about the service yards, postponed while every wagon hauled harvest or lane stone. Jon told him to measure the grain yard and stable court and price them for 128. The rest could stay unpaved until somebody showed him a cart lost in them.
