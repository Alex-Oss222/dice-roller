## Turn 13 | 129/02/02 | Evening
Elapsed: Two days, from the evening of 129/01/30.

Jon dismissed the steward until the evening of the second day of the new moon. The second roll could wait. He sent the maester to fetch the military returns and spent the following day with those, the stores accounts and the northern roads laid out before him.

The lords had reported thirty-one thousand four hundred men fit to answer a summons. The newer horse roll named two thousand two hundred and sixty men with horse and mail, already part of northern strength, not another host to add to it. There was no equally useful current account of ships and crews. The grain papers needed bringing forward from their separate counting dates before anyone could promise supplies at the Moat.

The chief clerk matched each house’s return to its covering letter, copied its date, and entered the named horsemen beside their home holdings. He ruled separate columns for men retained, men proposed for the road and men assigned to ships. A split pen lay in the ink tray.

When the maester returned, Jon gave him the orders.

“No more than twelve thousand altogether. The greater part of each lord’s men stays at home. They are to look after their lands, not empty them. Small companies to Moat Cailin, gradually, ready by the fourth moon. No march beyond it yet.”

The date was written as the first day of the fourth moon. Each house was to return its proposed company, provisions and departure date; the departures would be allotted against one northern total, with the shipmen counted in it. A lord could not send an extra company merely because his neighbour had done so. The nearer houses could prepare first while the more distant summonses travelled.

The stores officer brought his paper into the solar before the letters were sealed.

“The three towers will not house them,” he said. “The carpenter’s order was to mend the road and towers by summer. I need his word about camping ground and carts before the companies begin arriving.”

A request for that report went with the Moat’s instructions. The houses were to state what food and transport their detachments could bring. The existing repairs continued; an army’s camp and supply account would have to be reckoned separately.

The coastal letters went to Lord Desmond, Locke and the Flint of Widow’s Watch, with the same instructions carried onward to the other eastern coastal holdings. Dragonstone lay on that side of Westeros, far south of the Bite. Jon ordered ships and men made ready, local patrols strengthened, and armed vessels serving the Triarchy watched and turned away where the patrols could manage it. He did not order the fleet south.

“Patrolling the Bite will not close the Gullet,” the maester said. “Manderly will need a further order for that voyage.”

The letters requested hulls, crews, carrying room and the time needed to make each ship ready. They did not proclaim a blockade of distant waters or order the seizure of every merchant from Lys, Myr and Tyrosh. Manderly would receive an answer about preparations, but not yet the answer he had asked for about the succession.

“That is the last copy,” the maester said.

The clerk still had Karhold’s to finish. Once it was done, the maester read the copies against the original, folded the riders’ packets and fitted the shorter messages for the ravens. The common notice went out with the principal lords’ orders on the morning of the second day; riders took the messages that could not go directly by bird. No coastal acknowledgement had returned by evening.

Arra came to the solar after the officers had left. Jon told her privately what he remembered of the war: the prince killed after going to Storm’s End, the trap at Rook’s Rest, Vhagar burning the river country, and the betrayals at Tumbleton. These were histories from his former life, not reports arriving at Winterfell.

“At the Gullet, ships from the Three Daughters catch Rhaenyra’s younger sons on their way to Pentos. One escapes. The other is taken. Jacaerys dies in the fighting afterward.”

“On his dragon?”

“He falls into the sea. The accounts differ over what brought the dragon down. Crossbowmen kill him in the water.”

She asked where the northern men had been. He told her about the winter volunteers and the later host, and about the ruin that remained when the fighting over the throne was nearly finished. He did not give her a certain account of what would now happen.

“I would not send our men to those battles just because you know their names,” she said. “And I would tell Manderly about the ships before he sails. He can ask his captains what can be done. I cannot.”

Jon asked whether she wanted to come south with him or remain in the North as his regent.

“I trust no one more.”

“I want to stay here,” she said. “I don’t want to fight in the south.”

She asked whether the steward would remain, and whether the captains would still hold their commands. Jon’s offer did not remove them. She would have their service and counsel, not a household emptied for the campaign.

“Then I’ll do it. While you’re still here, I want to sit with the steward and learn what I’ll have to answer for. But tell the captains yourself before you go. Put it in writing for the lords. I won’t have them sending every question south because they think you only asked me to mind the rooms.”

Her acceptance settled where she wished to serve; the appointment still needed its writ and public declaration before Jon’s departure. She left the question of the ships to Manderly and returned to the proposed host. “You’ve told me what happened to them. What are you going to ask for before our men help put one of them on the throne?”
