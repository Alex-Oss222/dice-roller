## Turn 11 | Thirtieth day of the twelfth moon, 128 AC | Evening

Elapsed: A hundred and fifty days, from the evening of the thirtieth day of the seventh moon to the evening of the thirtieth day of the twelfth moon.

### Harvest

He answered the stores officer's question in the first week of the eighth moon, before a sickle had been put to anything, because the answer decided what the carts did.

Twelve thousand quarters, rye and oats, bought at the harvest price from the tenants of his own twelve estates first and from Cerwyn's and Hornwood's tenants after, at the book's price and no more, paid in coin at the granary door against the sealed quarter, and carted to the six castle granaries and the four rural ones as the stores officer directed. The stores officer said that twelve thousand would fill the new room and leave the old, and that the tenants would sell at that price gladly because White Harbor's buyers paid less and paid later, and that he would need forty carts for six weeks and four of Hobb's boys. He had them. Jon told him that the grain was a reserve against a bad year and not for sale, that it was to be turned and counted like the rest, and that if anyone asked what the bad year was he was to say the next one.

The harvest was good and dry, the best of the three. The lord's share came up the approach and into the six granaries in the grain yard, which was paved now and drained to its gutters, and the bought grain came after it on the forty carts with a clerk at every door and the price paid in silver into each tenant's hand against the cut tally. The four rural granaries filled from their own villages and took bought grain on top. The Knott's store went up at his valley mouth in the eleventh moon on the timber marked in the Norrey's wood, and six hundred quarters went up to it on pack beasts under Donnel's successor before the snow, and the Knott put his lock on it as the Norrey had.

The count on the twentieth of the tenth moon was seventy-eight thousand six hundred quarters in store, the bought grain in it, against sixty-nine thousand eight hundred the year before; the loss between the doors and the count was four hundred and twenty quarters, the heaps having been turned in dry air, and the stores officer said the figure was now a figure and not a guess and that in a wet year it would be three times that and he would know why. Twenty thousand two hundred quarters of it stood under the castle's roofs, the three old granaries and the six new, which was more than Winterfell had ever kept under its own roof, and the stores officer, who had said two granaries in 126, said that he had been right in 126 and was right now and that the difference was the king.

The lords' harvest accounts came in with the grain, naming this year every man with horse and mail of his own and where he lived, as the order had said. The clerk made them into a book and called it the horse roll, and the book said that two thousand two hundred and sixty such men lived in the North by their lords' own naming, and where. Jon read it through once and locked it with the other sheet.

### The wedding

Lord Cerwyn's daughter married Lord Hornwood's heir at Castle Cerwyn on the twelfth day of the ninth moon, with the harvest in, and Jon rode down for it with Arra, who had come down for the harvest feast and said she might as well see a Cerwyn wedding since she had been counted at so many Cerwyn suppers.

It was a wedding under the heart tree in Cerwyn's godswood and a feast in his hall that went on until the candles were stubs, with Hornwood's people and Cerwyn's and Tallhart, who had come for it, and Glover, who had not been expected and had come, and the two new houses eating off one board. Jon gave the bride a sealed measure set of her own, which the hall laughed at and which Lady Cerwyn, who counted days, understood before anyone. Lord Cerwyn said nothing about heirs that night and looked at Jon only once, and the look was not unkind.

The lords talked of the king when the women had gone up, because they could not help it. Hornwood had Lord Desmond's letters and so had everyone, and Tallhart asked straight out whether the North would be called south when the king died, and for whom. Jon said that the king was alive, that the North kept the king's peace while he was, and that when he was not Winterfell would say what it would do before it did it, to every house at once, and that nobody at that table would read it first in a letter from White Harbor. Glover said that was what Winterfell had said to him by saying nothing, and that he had understood it. Cerwyn said he would like it in writing. Jon said he would have it in writing when there was something to write.

### Defects

He sent the carpenter and the reeve round every building and every yard of the roll's work in the eleventh and twelfth moons, with the clerk and a list, to look for what three winters had done, because the roll's closing examination was two moons away and he did not mean to be told at it what he could have been told now.

The list came back longer than the carpenter had wanted and shorter than the reeve had feared. The market hall's roof leaked at the north valley where the carpenter's own man had laid the lead short, and the carpenter mended it at his own charge without being asked and wrote that down himself. The approach's gutters had silted for a hundred yards below the gate and the water had begun to stand on the crown; they were dug out in a day and would need digging out every autumn. The square's setts had heaved at the corner by the well where the frost got under them, a yard square, and were lifted and relaid on fresh sand. Two of the rural wells had broken windlasses, which the villages had not mended because nobody had told them it was theirs to mend, and Jon told them. The winter hall's privy drain had blocked and been cleared by the keeper with a rod, twice. The tannery's upper well had gone brackish in the dry summer and would need deepening. Tallhart wrote that the deck planks of his first bridge were worn at the wheel lines after two years and asked whose charge the planks were, and was sent the agreement's page number, and replaced them.

Jon read the list in the solar with the steward and said that the roll had built forty things and that forty things needed keeping, and that the keeping was not in the roll. He appointed a town works keeper for Winter Town, a mason's journeyman who had worked on the hall, at one hundred and sixty dragons a year from the fair's fee, to walk the paving and the roofs and the wells every moon and mend what a journeyman could and report what he could not; and he had the clerk write a page at the end of the works book headed with each building's name and what it needed each year and who paid, which the steward said was the first time anyone had written down the cost of owning a thing rather than the cost of making it.

### The tenth moon

The second instalment came in from the tenth and was counted under three keys. The Crown’s share went south on the twenty-seventh. Lord Desmond’s receipt for the spring chests came back with the lighter count, which was up again.

The Hand's letter came in the twelfth moon under the Hand's seal and in the Hand's clerk's hand, addressed to the Warden of the North. The king was gravely ill. The realm's peace was to be kept in every part of it. The Wardens were to hold their levies ready to the king's call and to report to the Hand any disturbance of the peace in their marches. It said nothing about the succession and nothing about the princess, and in saying nothing it said what the court was. Jon answered it the same day in his own hand, in four lines: the North kept the king's peace and would keep it; the North's levies answered the king and would answer him; the North was quiet; and the Warden of the North commended himself to His Grace and prayed for his health. The maester read it before it was sealed and said it was an answer a man could read either way. Jon said that was the kind of answer the question deserved.

The Knott's poles were counted at the first snow by the patrol that went up to the stone, and thirty-eight of the forty stood in their places, and two did not, and a rider went up to the Knott with the count and nothing else, and the two were standing within the week. Jon did not go up.

The tannery's second cycle came out of the pits in the eleventh moon: seventy-four hides of leather from eighty, sold at Barrowton, which the works book set against the yard's three years of cost and found wanting, as it had found the first cycle. The journeyman asked for twenty more pits. Jon refused them. He said the roll had said that the money for the other pits would be released when the figures supported it and that the figures did not, and that the journeyman could have twenty pits when seventy-four hides a cycle paid his wage and the bark, and not before.

Orro's looms gave seven thousand six hundred yards in the year against the eight thousand signed for, and the Braavosi factor took every yard the mill had fulled and did not quarrel about the four hundred. The works book said what it had said for three years: that the grey earned in coin a little less than it cost in wages, wool and Orro, and that the nine weavers who now bought yarn at their own risk, four more having come in from Cerwyn's villages, earned more from the same yarn than Stark did. Jon kept the works at the same scale for the remainder of Orro’s contract and told the steward that at the closing examination he wanted the cloth's three years on one sheet with nothing left off it, and that if the sheet said what he thought it would say he would know what to do with the looms.

### The twelfth moon

The steward began the charge and discharge for the roll's closing in the second week of the twelfth moon, with the clerk and the two school boys who had been in the works account two years now, and the solar table was covered with it for the rest of the moon: what each division had been allowed, what it had actually cost, what other lords and tenants had truly delivered, which buildings stood and who kept them, what the fairs and the cloth and the fish and the leather had earned in coin, what was still owed and to whom, and what remained in the vault. The steward said that the account would be ready for the first moon of 129 as the roll required and that it would not be pretty in every column. Jon said he had not built it to be pretty.

Arra stayed at Winterfell for the winter, saying the Norrey's hall was full of the Knott's sheep, which it was not, and took the rooms over the Hunter's Gate that she had refused in 126, which nobody remarked on. Bennard was told at the month's end that his son was well. The maester reported that the king's health, by every letter that reached White Harbor, was not expected to see the spring.

On the evening of the thirtieth the bought grain was paid for, the Crown’s share had gone south and only retentions remained on the roll’s current contracts. The steward asked whether the examination should be held in the solar or the hall. Jon said the hall, with every officer of the roll present and the doors open.
