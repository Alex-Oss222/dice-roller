## Turn 6 | Thirtieth day of the second moon, 127 AC | Evening

Elapsed: A hundred and twenty days, from the evening of the thirtieth day of the tenth moon of 126 to the evening of the thirtieth day of the second moon of 127.

### Fire

The night watch on the south wall saw the glow in the second hour after midnight on the eighteenth day of the eleventh moon and rang the gate bell, and Jon was in the yard with his boots unlaced before the second peal.

It was in the low lane behind the old market, where the chandler rendered his tallow in a shed against the back wall of his house, and by the time the first garrison men came down the approach at a run the shed was gone and the chandler's thatch was burning and the thatch of the house beyond it had caught at the eaves. The lane was eleven feet wide. The wind was from the west, which was toward the two new warehouses and the market hall.

The reeve had the fire point at the lane's end open before the garrison arrived: the cask unlidded, the ten buckets off their pegs, the ladder and the hooks down. He had thirty people in a line from the cask to the chandler's house passing buckets, and the cask was half empty, and the bucket line was doing what bucket lines did to a burning thatch, which was nothing. Jon took the line off the burning house and put it on the next one, wetting the thatch on the windward side, and put the ladder and the hooks on the house beyond that. Four garrison men went up the ladder and the hooks went into the thatch at the ridge and the men on the ropes below pulled it down into the lane in smoking slabs, and the roof timbers stood bare and did not burn, having nothing on them to burn. The second cask was brought from the square on a handcart and the line went on from it. The chandler's house went, and the two beyond it, where the thatch had caught before the hooks came. The fourth house kept its bare rafters. The warehouses, forty yards downwind, took sparks on their shingles all night and a man on each roof with a wet sack, and in the morning the shingles were scorched in a dozen places and whole.

Nobody died. The chandler burned both hands pulling his rendering copper out of the shed and was the first man treated in the new infirmary, by the reeve's wife, there being no chirurgeon yet. Three families lodged in the winter hall, which had been built for strangers and took its own townspeople first.

Jon held the inquiry in the market hall the next morning with the reeve, the watch officer and the chandler's neighbors. The copper had been left on the fire with the shed door shut. The chandler said he had banked it. Jon ordered the three houses rebuilt by their owners at their own charge, as the custom was, but with shingle and not thatch, and the lane widened by the width of the burned shed, which the chandler could not have back; a second cask at each point within sixty paces of the warehouses, the hall and the weavers' houses; and a rule, cried in the square, that no rendering, brewing or forge fire in Winter Town was to be left banked in a closed shed overnight, on a fine of the fire casks' price. The reeve said the townspeople would hate it. Jon said they would hate it less than the chandler's neighbors hated him.

### The cold moons

The winter hall kept sixty people through the twelfth and first moons, carters caught by the snow, the three burned-out families, a party of Umber men who had brought their lord's grain to the Hornwood landing and could not get home, and the usual drunks, under a keeper who had been the gaoler at the east gate and knew how to count heads at night. The chirurgeon came in the first moon, a barber-surgeon called Lew who had served eleven years on Lord Desmond's ships and had been put ashore for drinking, which Lord Desmond's letter said plainly and Jon took on its own terms. He set a carter's leg in his first week and lost a child to a winter cough in his second, and kept both in a book.

The school kept its fourteen boys. Two could cut a true tally by midwinter and read it back; the rest could not, and Hobb said that was the ordinary proportion and that the two were the reeve's son and a tenant's boy from the Upper White Knife, and that he would have the tenant's boy in the works account by summer if Jon would let him.

Lord Umber's letter came in the twelfth moon. One of his four granaries had sagged under the first heavy snow, the ridge had gone down a foot and the gable had split, and eighty quarters of barley in it were wet and spoiling, and it was Winterfell's green timber that had done it, and he would send his thirty-two thousand dragons when Winterfell had sent sound wood. Jon showed the letter to the carpenter, who had chosen and marked the timber. The carpenter said the timber had been winter-felled oak and pine, sound and seasoned the one year the roll allowed, and that no sound timber in the world would hold a roof pitched as flat as the Last Hearth pitched its roofs under four feet of snow, and that Umber's own carpenter had built the roof. Jon wrote to Lord Umber that his carpenter would ride north at the thaw to look at the four roofs with Umber's carpenter beside him and say before both of them what had failed, and that until then the thirty-two thousand was owed and the roof was Umber's. He did not expect the letter to be welcome and it was not answered.

Benjen had a cough in the twelfth moon that went to his chest, and the maester bled him and sat with him and he mended. Bennard was told at the month's end, as agreed, that his son had been sick and was well. Margaret's letters came and went through the maester and said nothing the maester thought Jon needed to read.

The cloth came off the looms through the winter, which was when the spinners spun and the weavers wove in any northern house. By the end of the second moon six thousand eight hundred yards had been woven and foot-fulled. Berrick's man passed five thousand two hundred for the Braavos grey and rejected the rest for the fulling, as before. The works book, which Jon had kept honestly on purpose, said that the first season had earned barely half its wages and the raw value of its wool, and that the five weavers at their own risk had done better than Stark had, by selling at the hall. The fuller said again that it was the feet. Jon kept the second season at three thousand stone, not the roll's six, and ordered the mill.

The mill was priced in the first moon by a millwright from White Harbor who looked at the lord's grain mill below the town and said that the race would carry a second wheel on the far bank with a new sluice, and that the fulling stocks, the cams and the hammers were a trade he knew, and that the whole would be eighteen moons at the price he put before them. Jon took it, and the sluice timber was cut.

The tannery began in the eleventh moon before the hard frost, below the town on the fall of the ground where the reeve had wanted it, with a journeyman tanner from Barrowton who had left one of Dustin's six yards for Stark's wage. He dug ten pits, not fifty, lined them with clay and oak planks, and said he would take forty hides in the third moon and that nobody would see leather before the year's end, and that if Lord Stark had been told otherwise he had been told by a man who had never tanned. The shed was let. The two wells would wait for the thaw.

Dustin's road and Glover's stopped at the frost with four miles and two miles made. Master Wyl wrote from White Harbor that he had looked at Locke's landing on his way south and that it would take a sixty-foot timber wharf at the book's price plus two hundred for the approach, where the gravel bank shelved, and that he was cutting voussoirs by his fire as promised and would be at the Acorn Water with them on the first cart after the thaw.

### The fair

The fair opened on the fifteenth day of the second moon with the market square paved under it and the hall roofed over it and nobody certain whether anyone would come.

They came. Cerwyn's tenants came first because they were nearest, over the new bridge, and then Tallhart's and Hornwood's and the Winter Town people themselves, and on the third day the Norrey came down the Flint's stone track with Arra and the Wull and two hundred sheep and forty packs of hill wool, which no clan had ever brought to a Stark fair in anyone's memory, because there had never been a reason to. Berrick came up from White Harbor with salt, wine, iron and three carts of Braavosi goods, and brought with him a Braavosi called Orro who had woven in Braavos for twenty years and had been sent north by the house Lord Desmond had written to, to see whether the North's wool was worth his time. Karstark's steward came with the first yarn from the Karhold spinning house, which the five independent weavers bought before noon. Dustin did not come but sent his steward with twenty-five thousand dragons in a chest and thirty horses from the river herds, which the stable master walked, trotted, looked in the mouth and valued one by one at the book price for a patrol horse, and wrote against the note.

The weighhouse decided more quarrels than the fair court did. The sealed set on its chain was used eleven hundred times in the fortnight by the weighmaster's count, and three times a day somebody's own weights were put beside it and found light, and most of those quarrels ended at the scale. The fair court under the steward sat each afternoon in the hall and heard fourteen matters in the fortnight: nine on weights and measures, two purses cut, one horse sold lame, one Bolton man and one Norrey man who had fought over a sheep that belonged to neither, and a Hornwood chapman caught with a false stone weight whom the court fined and whose goods it held until the fine was paid. Jon sat on the last day to hear the appeals, of which there were two, and upheld the court in both.

He had the lords who had come to supper on the fourth night: Cerwyn, Tallhart, Hornwood, the Norrey and the Wull at one table in the great hall, and the mead and the castle's ale, and Berrick and Orro at the end because Jon wanted them to hear the lords and the lords to hear them. Tallhart wanted his other three bridges, or the one that his tenants asked about most, and Jon said the one, from the public roll, if his maester would swear the site. Hornwood had six kilns and two hundred gallons of tar in barrels and wanted a second buyer in case Lord Desmond's shipwrights tired of him, and Berrick said he would not tire. The Norrey asked what a stone bridge cost, and was told, and said the hills would have poles. Orro said, through Berrick, that northern wool would never make broadcloth and would make a grey that sold in every port of the narrow sea if it were fulled by a mill and finished by someone who knew how, and that he would stay two years at four hundred dragons a year and his keep, which was six times what a weaver got and about what a weaving master got in Braavos. Jon took him at the table.

Cerwyn waited until the mead had gone round twice and then said, in front of the others, that his daughter was fifteen and would be sixteen in the sixth moon, and that he would not say it twice. Jon said he would not marry this year, nor the next, and would say the same to every lord at this table and every lord who was not at it, and that Lord Cerwyn should hear it from him before he heard it from anyone else. Cerwyn said he had heard it, and drank, and asked Tallhart about his bridge.

The reeve closed his tally with more trade than the roll had expected. The scale fees covered the fair’s watchmen, porters, weighmaster and clerk and left money over. The steward asked whether the Crown’s share should go with the next instalment. Jon said it should, and told the clerk to report the new fair to the king’s treasurer himself.

The arch and mill accounted for most of the work still under contract. The carpenter would ride to the Last Hearth at the thaw. Jon told the steward he meant to follow him north by the Dreadfort, the Last Hearth and Karhold, then return through the Hornwood. Bolton’s first thirty thousand dragons were due in the fourth moon, and Jon wanted to be in his hall when they were counted.
