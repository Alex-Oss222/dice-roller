## Turn 10 | Thirtieth day of the seventh moon, 128 AC | Evening

Elapsed: A hundred and thirty-five days, from the evening of the fifteenth day of the third moon to the evening of the thirtieth day of the seventh moon.

### The spring

The paviours lifted the stable court on the sixth day of the fourth moon and broke a pipe nobody living had known was there. The roll had said to survey the buried drains and the hot-water runs before any yard was lifted, and the carpenter had surveyed what the castle's old plans showed, and the old plans had not shown a clay pipe a foot under the court that carried warm water from the springs in the walls to the stable troughs and had done since some Stark had laid it who had not thought to write it down. The paviour's pick went through it and the stable court filled to the ankle with water that steamed, and the horses were walked out into the approach while the castle's own masons found the run and cut it and capped it and laid a new length of pipe around the court's edge where the paving would not sit on it. It took eleven days, and the stable master said the troughs had been warm all his life and he had supposed it was the stable.

Everything else went as the roll had said it would when the roll was right. The arch took its first spring flood on its own feet, lower than the year before, and Master Wyl's retention was paid to White Harbor on the carpenter's sworn sheet that nothing had moved. The side lanes were finished in the fifth moon, fourteen thousand square yards of rubble cobbles from the square to the last house, and the reeve walked them in the rain with his knotted line and found no puddle he could not step over. The Hornwood landing had its deck on by the end of the fifth moon and the lighters loaded at the wharf at any state of the water, and Lord Hornwood's first letter about it was to ask who paid for the piles when the ice took them, and the steward wrote back with the agreement's page number.

The fourth-moon instalment came closer to full payment than any in Jon’s time. The Dreadfort’s second thirty thousand dragons arrived in a separate sack. The Crown’s share went south on the twenty-seventh with its part of the fair and the clerk’s letter.

Lord Desmond's letter of the sixth moon said what Jon had been waiting two years to read in another man's hand. The king was failing. He had not left his bed since the fourth moon; the Hand governed openly; the queen's sons were at court and the princess's on Dragonstone, and the Hand's clerks had stopped asking White Harbor what its ships carried and started asking what its lord would do. Desmond had answered that his lord would do as Winterfell did. He asked, in the last line, that Winterfell tell him what that was before anyone else did.

Jon did four things in the week after the letter and told nobody what they were for, which deceived nobody who had read the same letter. He had the stores officer measure the castle's covered room again and say whether it was enough for a reserve above the ordinary year, and the stores officer said it was not, and that he had said two granaries in 126 because two were what the harvest wanted and that this was a different question; and Jon ordered four more of the roll's ten castle granaries, let in the fourth moon and to be roofed before the harvest. He told the captains to raise the garrison by a hundred men from the estates' sons over the summer, at the book's pay, and to drill them with the rest. He sent the road gang and the carpenter south to Moat Cailin under the repair order that had stood since 125 and never been fully executed, with timber and coin to put the causeway and the three standing towers in order by the next summer, which was not the roll's work and was not charged to it. And he wrote to every house that the harvest accounts of men were to name, this year, every man with horse and mail of his own and where he lived.

### Deepwood Motte

He rode west on the first day of the sixth moon with eight men to see Glover's road and Glover's bridge and the ward he had refused at the fair, and because he had not been to Deepwood Motte in Cregan's memory or his own.

The road through the Wolfswood was the six miles Glover had asked for and the two miles beyond that Glover had gravelled himself when the roll's six ran out short of his own gate, with the same ditches, because, as his reeve said, a road that stopped in a wood was a joke the Wolfswood would make at his lord's expense every spring. The bridge over the Wolfswood's black stream had been built after the harvest with Winterfell's carpenter watching and stood on four bents in water that never fell low, and Glover's men had driven the piles from a raft.

Deepwood Motte was a wooden castle on a wooded hill above the Bay of Ice, and Glover was a plain lord with a plain hall who kept Brandon Stark as he would have kept his own son, which was to say worked him. Brandon squired at supper, said nothing to Jon that a squire did not say, and kept the word he had given at the fair so exactly that Jon took note of it and said so to Glover, who said the boy had come to him sullen and was leaving sullenness behind him a piece at a time, and that in two years Jon would have a man to deal with and should think now about what to do with him. Jon said he was thinking.

Glover asked for a second bridge, on the road from the Motte toward Sea Dragon Point where his fishing villages carted their catch, and Jon granted it from the roll's inventory on the same terms and had the agreement sealed in the hall. He did not ask Glover about the king, and Glover did not ask him, which was Glover's way of saying he would do as Winterfell did.

He came home by Torrhen's Square and found that Master Tallhart had built a weighhouse, a plain one, at his own market, with the sealed set Winterfell had issued him on a chain inside it, and a weighmaster who was his own reeve's brother, and had cried it to his tenants as the only true weight in the west. Tallhart said he had got the idea from Winter Town and the scales from Winterfell and the rest from his own head, and asked whether that was permitted. Jon said that was what the sets had been issued for.

### The Flint's stone

Arra went up to the hills after the shearing, and before she went she told him, in the tack room where she had read the roll two years before, that the Knott had sent to her father asking the Norrey to stand with him against the Flint, because the Flint's men had burned a Knott shieling over a grazing, and the Knott's men had answered by pulling up forty of the Flint's road poles and driving off sixty of his sheep, and that the Norrey did not want to stand with either and wanted the Stark to come up to the Flint's stone at the summer meeting and say who was right, which was a thing no Stark had done in her father's lifetime. Jon said he would come. She said then he would have to walk the ledge, because she was not having him led over it on a garron like a sack.

He went up on the tenth day of the seventh moon with Sergeant Donnel's successor and six men, on garrons as far as the stone, and walked the ledge on foot with the garrons led behind him one at a time, as she had said he would, with the drop on his left hand and the poles the Flint's men had reset standing in their line above him. The Norrey met him at the top of it. The meeting was held on the fourteenth on the flat below the stone where the clans had met since there were clans, the Norrey, the Wull, the Liddle, the Harclay, the Burley and the Flint with their men behind them, and the Knott, who came last with sixty men and sat down apart, and had never, Arra said, come at all when the Flint was there.

Jon heard it standing, as they all stood. The Flint said the grazing was his and the shieling was on it and had been burned because it was on it. The Knott said the grazing had been his grandfather's and the shieling was his and the sheep and the poles were payment. Both were partly true and both had been true for sixty years, and nobody at the stone wanted a Stark to say whose the grazing was, because the next Stark would say otherwise. Jon did not say whose it was. He said that the shieling had been burned and that a burned house was paid for in the hills in sheep, and set it at twenty, which the Wull said aloud was the old price; that sixty sheep taken for a shieling worth twenty were forty too many and the forty were to go back; and that the poles were not the Flint's, they were the road's, and a man who pulled a road pole in the snow killed whoever came after him on that track, Knott or Flint or Stark, and that the Knott's men would set every one of the forty back in its place before the first snow, and that if one of them was missing at the first snow he would come up himself and ask why. The Knott said nothing for a long time, and then said that he had sent the Stark's letter back unopened because he had expected it to say what the Starks said, and asked what a store cost. Jon said it cost a clan its labor and Stark its timber and six parts of the rest, and the key was the clan's. The Knott said he would have one.

They drank at the Norrey's hall that night, the Norrey's own mead and not Winterfell's, and the Norrey said at his own table before the chiefs what Jon had said at Winterfell before the Wull, that the Stark had mead at his table and shelter at his hearth as long as he lived, and that he had not said it for a Stark before. Jon stayed three days. The Knott's store timber was marked in the Norrey's wood to be cut in the autumn, and Arra walked the ledge with him on the way down and then turned round at the stone and went back up.

### Summer

Donnel came back from Skagos in the seventh moon on Lord Desmond's cog, which had put him ashore at a stony cove on the island's south side with the letter and a gift of iron and brought him off again two days later. The chieftain of that part of the island had taken the letter without opening it, fed him, given him a stone axe with a handle of some beast's bone, and sent him back to the ship, and that was the whole of the embassy. Donnel laid the axe on the solar table and said the island had looked like a place that wanted nothing from anyone and would go on wanting it. Jon kept the axe.

The Flint of Flint's Finger had built twelve of his twenty fish stations on the advance by the fifth moon, cheaply and fast and on the beach, and a gale in the seventh moon took five of them into the sea with their racks and a season's salt. The Flint wrote that the advance should bear the loss. The steward wrote back, on Jon's instruction, that the advance had been against fish sold and not against gales, that the stations had been sited by the Flint's own reeve, and that Locke's, which stood above the tide line on his maester's advice, had lost nothing; and the Flint rebuilt the five higher at his own charge and said nothing more about it.

The mill fulled all spring. Orro's looms gave four thousand two hundred yards by the end of the seventh moon, which was slower than the winter because the spinners were in the fields, and the mill's fulling cut the Braavosi's rejects to one bolt in twenty. The five independent weavers complained to the gate court that Orro gave the yarn to the wage looms first and them second, and Jon ruled that the yarn was sold to them at the same priority as it was issued to his own, because the contract was for eight thousand yards and he did not care whose looms made them. The dismissed receiver's son petitioned for his father's holding and was refused, and the school boy who held its tally was confirmed in it at the book's pay with a grown clerk over him.

At the end of the seventh moon the steward kept the garrison’s hundred and the Moat Cailin repairs on their own accounts. The four new castle granaries stood framed with their roofs going on. The stores officer asked how much grain Jon meant to buy at harvest beyond the lord’s own share, at what price, and from whom.
