## Turn 14 | 129/03/01 | Evening
Elapsed: Twenty-nine days. Two weeks preparing Arra at Winterfell, followed by the journey to Moat Cailin.

### The terms

“I mean to bargain with Rhaenyra,” Jon told her. “Not send her twelve thousand men and ask what she will give us afterward.”

Arra drew the stool nearer the table. “Start with what happens to the men.”

“They stay under a commander appointed by Winterfell. No northern company is detached without my agreement. We settle the campaign’s purpose and its duration before they march. A queen may direct the war. She does not divide our men among her captains.”

“And if she will not agree?”

“That part does not change.”

He explained the two harbours separately. At White Harbor he wanted royal investment in building and repairing merchantmen and defensive ships: yards, slips, warehouses, cordage, seasoned timber, and skilled men to train northern apprentices. Manderly must be party to that settlement. Bear Island needed a defended landing, provisions, patrol boats and somewhere to repair them, not an expensive fleet without crews or work. The wharf and enclosure already promised to Mormont remained a separate gift, not something to withhold until a queen paid for it. He wanted the right to hire southern shipwrights and experienced naval officers and bring them north.

“Will she send those men while she needs her own ships?” Arra asked.

“That will have to be in the agreement. So will what she pays.”

“Then keep the names and the payment days in it. I would not know what to do with a letter promising us a shipyard.”

Trade required more than one seal. Jon wanted a royal charter covering royal ports, including Dragonstone, and a separate bargain with Corlys for Driftmark, Hull and Spicetown. Northern merchants would seek reduced charges, secure access to warehouses and markets, resident factors and settled procedures for disputes. In return, southern merchants would receive reciprocal access, with northern timber, wool, hides and provisions available through trade rather than promised away as free supplies.

He also wanted a perpetual royal right to charter two additional northern cities, their sites approved by Winterfell and the lords who held the land. Winter Town came first; Barrowton was his preferred second, with Dustin’s agreement. A surveyed mainland harbour on the Bay of Ice remained a possible alternative, not a third charter. The grant would expressly cover markets, annual fairs, merchant associations, town courts, warehouses, fortification and specified local dues.

“Will Dustin still hold his town?”

“Yes.”

“And the people here? Will they owe more because someone calls this a city?”

“The charter permits named dues. It is not an order raising them.”

The Gift took longer. Jon wanted recognition of Stark ownership and administration of both Brandon’s Gift and the New Gift, with six tenths of the defined proceeds going to the Watch and four tenths to Winterfell.

“Both?” Arra asked. “Not just the land Alysanne added?”

“Both.”

“The black brothers hold it now. Have they asked you to take it?”

“No. This is what I mean to seek in the settlement.”

“Then you need their answer as well as hers. Six tenths of what? Before you pay the reeves, or afterward?”

They had not fixed the deductions or the treatment of grain, labour and coin. Arra wanted the Watch’s share stated so that a receiver could not shrink it by charging every northern expense against it. She did not agree that royal recognition alone would make the black brothers surrender their land. Jon’s demand remained in the proposed terms. No order went north to occupy the Gift.

The maester later made a fair copy of the negotiating brief. Neither Rhaenyra nor Corlys had received it. The steward’s proposals for a broader second works roll stayed unsigned.

### The fortnight

Hobb came each morning from the school. He taught Arra the forms she would actually use, with the steward’s settled accounts beside them. Jon spent the afternoons with her among petitions, supply returns and the officers responsible for carrying out orders.

“I know which is the receipt,” she said on the first morning.

She selected the warrant authorizing payment. Hobb placed the carter’s signed acknowledgement beside it.

“This says he was to be paid. This says he was.”

He laid out the matching halves of a tally, fitted their cut edges together and checked the notches against the written entry. Arra copied the payer, purpose, sum and date into the ruled columns. He covered the completed entry and gave her another pair.

They moved on to arrears, goods issued from stores, leases, appeals and letters requiring an immediate answer. The steward showed her what he could settle himself and what had always come to the lord. The stores officer took her through the granaries. The captains showed her the watch roster, the gate orders and the arrangements keeping Bennard and Benjen under separate guard. Their sentences and the wards’ oaths were not reopened.

Jon spoke to her about the people as well as the offices.

“The steward knows the accounts. The stores officer knows what has actually come through his doors. Listen to both when their papers disagree. The maester can tell you what a letter says; he does not decide what answer you owe. Ask the captains about their men. Ask Manderly about ships, but remember that he also speaks for White Harbor.”

“And my father?”

“Listen to him. Check his claims as you would another lord’s.”

She brought him a petition on the next day with three questions written beneath it, rather than asking him to decide it for her. He worked through the questions with her. Later, when the fair opened, she sat beside the steward while he heard a quarrel over the space allowed to a wool seller. She asked to see the stall agreement before hearing the seller a second time.

“Trust your judgment,” Jon told her. “I have faith in you. Take advice, examine what can be examined, and then decide. You do not need a raven from me to do the work I am leaving you.”

“I shall still write when I need to.”

“Yes.”

By the end of the fortnight she could follow the ordinary business and find the person who could answer a question she could not. Hobb would continue working with her after Jon left; the steward and captains would remain at their posts.

On the sixteenth day Jon named her regent before the assembled household. The writ gave her authority to govern in his absence until his return: to hear petitions and customary cases, direct northern defenses, issue stores, pay established charges and command the officers serving Winterfell. The field host and negotiations with royal claimants remained Jon’s business. Existing judgments and standing orders remained in force.

The maester read the writ aloud. The steward and captains acknowledged it before witnesses; copies went to the northern houses, with instructions that her lawful orders were to be obeyed as Jon’s. The distant lords would learn of it as the messages reached them.

### South

The earlier summonses brought answers during those two weeks. The nearer houses named their first companies and the food and transport they would bring. The clerk cleared their departures against the single northern ceiling, retaining the greater part of each house’s strength at home.

Manderly’s first return counted six armed coasters fit to sail and four needing work. His local patrols had begun. Larger carrying vessels belonged to merchants whose terms would need agreement before they could be taken south. He reported no encounter with an armed Triarchy vessel. His letter still asked which claimant Winterfell would support; Jon’s private intention to negotiate with the Blacks was not yet a declaration sent to the lords.

Jon left on the seventeenth with forty mounted men from the Stark contingent and packhorses carrying provisions and the travelling papers. The rest of the first companies went by their allotted departures. Arra remained at Winterfell with the household officers and Hobb.

The journey took fifteen days. They crossed the Acorn Water by the completed arch, used the patrolled kingsroad and took fodder at the arranged stops. Jon’s copies of the terms travelled with him. No envoy had been dispatched to Dragonstone, and no order had sent the eastern ships beyond their local patrols.

At Moat Cailin, the new companies camped on the firmer ground north of the ruins. Men unloaded sacks onto timber dunnage, covered them with oiled cloth and led the pack animals away from the drinking-water barrels. The carpenter still had work on the towers and causeway. The first camp used company tents and labour; it was not a finished lodging for the whole proposed host.

The muster clerk brought Jon the names: six hundred Stark men, including his escort, four hundred from Cerwyn and two hundred from Tallhart. Twelve hundred newly summoned men were present, besides the Moat’s existing guard. Four thousand places had been allotted on the land roll; the other companies had not yet reached the Moat. The storekeeper counted two hundred and forty quarters of grain remaining for the new companies. Their captains had brought the agreed initial provisions; the larger muster and longer service would require further deliveries.

Jon’s quarters had been put beside the command tent, where the officers could bring their returns. The clerk entered the escort once, struck its separate travelling subtotal, and set out the houses’ expected arrival dates for Jon to examine.
