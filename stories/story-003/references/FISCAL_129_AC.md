# The North in 129 AC

At the close of Turn 12, **Winterfell holds 16,576,000 gold dragons**. Stark’s ordinary annual receipts are planned at **5,829,820 GD**, leaving **4,509,679 GD after running costs and the Crown**, before new building. The North’s annual output is estimated at **67.7 million GD**, up from a comparable **66 million in 126**. White Harbor remains its commercial centre; most northerners still live from land, livestock and seasonal work.

## Money and the comparison

This revision uses **twenty times the former monetary scale** throughout Story 3. A former 1,000-dragon payment is now 20,000; a former one-penny price is twenty pennies. Wages, goods, debts, budgets, rents and reserves all change together. This is an author’s revision, not a windfall, debasement or twentyfold growth inside the story. Coin ratios remain 210 stags to a dragon and 56 pennies to a stag. People, grain, distances, dates and percentages do not multiply.

The [126 report](FISCAL_126_AC.md) and original works proposal retain their original monetary scale, marked at their openings. Their amounts convert once by ×20. The turn narratives have been trimmed of routine arithmetic; all monetary figures they retain, the backstory, WORLD, CHARACTER and the [revised price book](North_126AC_Price_Book.xlsx) already use the new scale. The original scene accounts underlying the reconciliation remain in Git history at `5a5aadc`. The price book retains its filename to preserve links. The 487 life history and other campaigns are separate.

The increase **within** the revised scale is much smaller and comes from production and trade. No complete annual trading ledger survives for 126. Replacing the old model’s tribute and rents with the amounts actually collected gives a comparable 126 receipt estimate of **5,557,400 GD**: 5,672,640 modeled receipts − 3,583,620 modeled tribute − 955,620 modeled rents + 3,492,000 collected tribute + 932,000 collected rents. The 129 plan is **272,420 higher, or 4.90%**. Against the model’s already optimistic ordinary year, the increase is **157,180, or 2.77%**. Neither comparison is a claim that the whole 126 income was counted.

## What has changed on the ground

Winterfell has six new granaries, three wells, paved working yards, standard measures, an account room and a school producing clerks. Winter Town has grown from 500 to 560 households. Its market hall, weighhouse, two warehouses, paved streets, ten wells, twenty privies, fire points, school, infirmary and winter hall make it a more useful place to live and trade. The two baths burn wood. The fulling mill, six looms and twenty-pit tannery remain small concerns; the early cloth and leather accounts lost money.

On Stark’s wider lands, eighteen wells, four granaries, a wool house, four timber bridges and road poles serve scattered settlements. Other lords received one stone bridge, eight timber bridges, twenty-six road miles, two wharves, fourteen wells and nine granaries. Four clan stores hold transferred grain. Lords also paid for works of their own, including Hornwood’s kilns, Bolton’s granaries and further roads under Dustin and Glover. The programme did not reach every village.

These works save time and stock and make existing markets easier to reach. The income plan also reflects the earlier reforms: accurate measures, dependable collection and better use of White Harbor. It does not attribute all northern growth to Jon’s buildings. Much of the gain is ordinary recovery and trade during three years without a northern war.

Winterfell’s household mainly consumes and administers produce from those estates; taxes received there are not a second GDP. The North’s 1.7 million increase is a **2.58% gain over three years**. A reasonable ordinary-year range is 66.5–69 million; the working figure is not a census. Grain losses were lower in the dry 128 harvest than in wet 127, but buildings cannot claim the whole weather difference. Sixty extra town households do not establish extra northern population: some are movers.

His descriptions support a fertile, populous Reach, wealthy western mines, rich river country and a much more difficult northern environment.[1] Total output and wealth per person are different measures.

The old Dorne and Iron Islands comparisons were too large. Their original 7.5 and 3.4 million become **48 and 18 million after correcting regional scale and applying the common monetary revision**, not 150 and 68 million. This revises both comparison years; it is not an economic collapse. Dorne’s small population and the islands’ restricted agricultural base warrant caution.[2][3]

No southern development ledger exists in the saved turns. The increases outside the North therefore assume modest ordinary growth in farming, crafts and commerce, roughly 1–4% across three years. They are not invented completed projects. The final column allocates a total **1.06 million GD of additional value added** from trade connected to northern demand and supply: southern salt, iron, cloth, food and wine; northern timber, wool, hides and fish; and the hauling, warehousing and finishing around them. The Crownlands and Reach receive the largest allowances for their markets and supply base. Smaller gains reach the Vale, river routes and western ports; Dorne and the islands receive much less.

That allocation assumes roughly 3 million GD of additional two-way merchandise turnover across the routes by 129, only a fraction of which becomes local wages and trading or manufacturing income. The cargo’s full price is not added again at every port. More northbound supplies cost northerners money; the same voyage can earn carriers and reduce empty returns. Southern wool producers can face more competition while their weavers gain cheaper or better supply. Gross demand and every trader’s profit do not move together. These are linked planning assumptions to revise when actual cargo accounts appear, not automatic multipliers on every house.

The stock line assumes about 6.7% more marketable volume and 4% better average realization than the original model, less wool diverted into yarn; it does not require a 10% rise in every herd. Grain sales imply about 254,300 quarters at the old average grain mix, against 236,735 in the model. That is a sale plan, subordinate to an adequate harvest and the retained reserve. Timber and market receipts have larger percentage gains from small bases; they require actual traffic and contracts, not a new tax demand. Take **5.56–5.98 million** as a useful ordinary-receipt range before a war or crop failure changes the case.

The extra hundred guards cost about 9,500 in coin plus 110 quarters of grain. The price book values their full support at about 9,532 including grain; the cash provision rounds upward from about 9,386. Their wage alone is about 7,573. The 2,680 keeping charge is additional to the original household establishment. No labour or maintenance becomes free because it is paid from another purse.

The former 3½-stag cloth contract is now **70 stags per yard**, or about **2,667 GD gross for 8,000 accepted yards**. The six loom rents become twenty stags each, about 0.57 GD in total. These remain small operations. Yarn at spinning cost plus a tenth does not recover the value of all raw wool and board; that concession remains in the accounts. Orro’s original two-year contract expires around 129/02.

Local harbour dues belong to the relevant coastal lord. Neither is another Stark receipt or an addition to GDP.

## Mormont and Reed: new orders for 129

The player has now authorized these gifts, after the closed roll. They are **reserved commitments at 129/01/30; no payment or construction is yet recorded**. They do not reopen the old programme or resolve the succession letter. Jon’s reason is private: remembrance of Lyanna Mormont’s support and of Bran and Meera. Their descendants’ future acts are not knowledge held by the lords of 129, nor a public condition of either grant.

**House Mormont: 180,000 GD.** Provide a working timber wharf of about sixty feet and a stone-walled refuge around the adjoining store and landing approach. The working scope is 120 yards of curtain, about nine feet high and four and a half feet thick, with a stout plain gate and wall walk. It protects people and stores from a raid; it is not a new stone castle or a walled island. Site choice must keep the store above storm water.

The wharf should take a working season once surveyed; the walls require roughly two or three working seasons with several gangs. Plan 100,000 cash in 129 and retain 80,000 for completion. Mormont keeps the buildings and allows about 3,000 yearly for ordinary repair and a small guard contribution from the existing household. No full gatehouse, towers or breakwater is included. A different site or major storm protection requires a new estimate. The old four fish stations and harbour granary remain separate existing works.

**House Reed: 50,000 GD.** Offer two raised stores of about 500 quarters each, two small landings of about twenty feet, eight shallow-draft boats, short removable timber approaches and an initial thousand quarters of food. The Reeds choose sites and keep the keys. This supports their people and their own movement through the Neck without laying a military road through their refuge or pretending Greywater Watch is a fixed masonry town. Boats are worked by existing local crews as needed, not a new permanent fleet establishment.

The smaller structures do not necessarily cost exactly half as much: the site and freight reserves cover this estimating shortcut. Plan 40,000 in 129 and retain 10,000 for completion or weather delay. Allow roughly 2,000 a year for upkeep, intermittent crew labour and handling under Reed’s household. The grant funds the first food purchase; no grain has yet left Stark’s counted stores. Completion is plausible during 129–130 after Reed agrees suitable sites, not immediately in winter.

These grants are gifts, with no repayment or tribute increase attached. No new income from them is put into the 129 base case. Their useful returns begin with safer landing, less spoilage and service to their people. Receiving-house upkeep enters those houses’ later accounts as the works open. The open question of a broader second roll remains with the player.
