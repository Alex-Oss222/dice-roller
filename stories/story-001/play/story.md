Ned keeps the meetings practical. He asks where each man's companies are quartered, when they last ate, how the watches are divided, whether the horses have been checked, whether the officers know where to find their own banners if the line shifts, and what worries the lord most about the morning. He does not ask for a speech. When drink is brought, he shares a cup and lets the other man talk.

The answers are not uniform. One lord is more concerned with tired men than the royal army. Too many of his rear companies have been awake since before dawn, first on movement and then on camp work. He says they are fresh enough, then asks whether Ned will permit him to change the next watch because half the men assigned to it have already served twice. Another lord wants cleaner messenger arrangements between neighboring commands. He has no wish to see a local success turn into a pursuit that leaves a flank open. A third is less worried about courage than about horses, ground near the river, and men trying to find their own officers once banners and dust begin to move.

Ned makes them separate the things they know from the things they have heard. No man is allowed to improve a scout's report merely because it has passed through three tents. They review the northern arrangements already in hand, who answers to whom, where orders are expected to come from, how men are to rally on their own command if noise or movement breaks contact, and which questions still depend on the final meeting with the other rebel leaders. Ned asks each lord what he thinks his own men will do badly if the day becomes confused. That gets longer answers than asking what they will do well.

He also asks about fear. Some of the answers are military. Men are afraid of being ridden down, losing sight of their banner, or getting trapped at the water. Some are simpler. They have wives north of the Neck, children who know only that their fathers marched south, fields left to brothers, and villages where the work will still need doing if the army wins. No single answer becomes a judgment on the host.

Before each lord leaves, Ned gives the same instruction in plain terms. Necessary watches stay manned. Necessary work gets finished. Everything else stops early enough for men to sleep.

"A tired army is a dead army," he tells one of them. "Do not have them sitting over cups half the night because they are frightened of lying down."

The order goes out through the northern command. It is not an order to abandon sentries, horses, armor checks, or duties that cannot wait. It is an order against making exhaustion into a point of pride.

Ned then goes to Robert.
