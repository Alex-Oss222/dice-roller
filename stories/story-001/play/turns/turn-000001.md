Jory comes when Ned sends for him. Ned does not call a general northern council. He tells Jory to bring the lords to him one at a time, with enough space between them that each man can speak without the next waiting inside the tent. Jory sends runners through the northern lanes of the camp and returns with the order understood. The first lord arrives while weapon work is still going on outside.

Ned keeps the meetings practical. He asks where each man's companies are quartered, when they last ate, how the watches are divided, whether the horses have been checked, whether the officers know where to find their own banners if the line shifts, and what worries the lord most about the morning. He does not ask for a speech. When drink is brought, he shares a cup and lets the other man talk.

The answers are not uniform. One lord is more concerned with tired men than the royal army. Too many of his rear companies have been awake since before dawn, first on movement and then on camp work. He says they are fresh enough, then asks whether Ned will permit him to change the next watch because half the men assigned to it have already served twice. Another lord wants cleaner messenger arrangements between neighboring commands. He has no wish to see a local success turn into a pursuit that leaves a flank open. A third is less worried about courage than about horses, ground near the river, and men trying to find their own officers once banners and dust begin to move.

Ned makes them separate the things they know from the things they have heard. No man is allowed to improve a scout's report merely because it has passed through three tents. They review the northern arrangements already in hand, who answers to whom, where orders are expected to come from, how men are to rally on their own command if noise or movement breaks contact, and which questions still depend on the final meeting with the other rebel leaders. Ned asks each lord what he thinks his own men will do badly if the day becomes confused. That gets longer answers than asking what they will do well.

He also asks about fear. Some of the answers are military. Men are afraid of being ridden down, losing sight of their banner, or getting trapped at the water. Some are simpler. They have wives north of the Neck, children who know only that their fathers marched south, fields left to brothers, and villages where the work will still need doing if the army wins. No single answer becomes a judgment on the host.

Before each lord leaves, Ned gives the same instruction in plain terms. Necessary watches stay manned. Necessary work gets finished. Everything else stops early enough for men to sleep.

"A tired army is a dead army," he tells one of them. "Do not have them sitting over cups half the night because they are frightened of lying down."

The order goes out through the northern command. It is not an order to abandon sentries, horses, armor checks, or duties that cannot wait. It is an order against making exhaustion into a point of pride.

Ned then goes to Robert.

He asks to speak with him before the larger command meeting, and the two are given the privacy available in a war camp. Ned begins as a friend. He asks Robert what he means to do when the fighting starts, not what the captains expect him to do.

Robert's answer is direct. He intends to be forward with his own men. He will not command the rebellion from a safe place behind everyone else. If Rhaegar comes within reach, Robert says, he will not turn away from him.

Ned lets him finish before answering.

"Then do not play the fool tomorrow," Ned says. "I will not have northern men die because you lose your head over one man. You are the man this rebellion follows. Be calm enough to remember that when the field turns ugly."

Robert's first answer is sharp. He asks whether Ned means to put him behind a baggage wagon while other men fight his war. Ned does not retreat from what he said. He tells Robert that fighting is not the same as throwing command away, and that a personal quarrel does not get to spend northern lives.

The argument does not become a break between them. Robert does not promise to stay out of danger. He does not promise to ignore Rhaegar if the prince is in front of him. He does say that he understands the point Ned is making: the army cannot be bent around his anger, and the other leaders need to know what he is doing before his personal choices create a hole they must fill.

Jon Arryn and Hoster Tully join them for the final coordination.

The four men work through command rather than speeches. They confirm which forces remain under which lord's authority and what information must move between them. They review how a messenger is to identify the order he carries, who sends another if the first does not arrive, and how neighboring commanders are to distinguish a local withdrawal from a general one. They go over rally and hold instructions and the danger of men chasing a broken enemy beyond supporting distance because the first part of a fight has gone well. No one produces a newly certain royal order of battle. Reports that remain reports are kept that way.

Jon presses hardest on cohesion. One contingent cannot assume another has seen the same movement or received the same message. Hoster wants the chain of messengers simple enough to survive losses and confusion. Robert accepts that his movements must be reported, even while he keeps his intention to fight forward. Ned confirms the northern officers will be told to treat signals and orders through recognized command rather than rumor moving along the line.

No wholesale reassignment of the northern host is made in the meeting. The clarifications can go out through runners without bringing the lords back into another long council.

Ned leaves the command meeting and walks the northern lines before he retires.

He does not inspect them as a parade. He stops where men are sitting with gear open, where horses are being checked, and where small groups have not yet broken for sleep. He asks names, homes, family, and what work they expect to return to. One man talks about nets and a father's boat. Another has brothers keeping a holding going without him. A younger man says his mother will complain about the state of his boots before she asks whether he was brave. Ned asks what they have been told about the morning and corrects only what needs correcting. Exact enemy numbers are still not known. The battle is expected, not already won.

The practical work continues around the conversations. A buckle is tightened and tested. A horse's forefoot is lifted, picked clean, and set down. A shield strap is pulled through its fastening and checked against the forearm. Men assigned to the next watch remain awake. Others begin to turn in under their captains' orders.

By the time Ned returns to his command tent, the long round of talking is finished. His field harness is still there for the morning. He has issued no battle action beyond the preparation and command instructions already given. At about half past ten he goes to bed. The expected battle remains for the next day, and nothing after Ned retires is resolved.
