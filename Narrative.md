# Research: Writing realistic medieval narratives, battles, combat, and human prose

## 1. Writing medieval historical narrative with the realism of nonfiction

The strongest approach is to treat the medieval world as a functioning society rather than a setting for a story. Characters should behave according to the material, political, religious, and social conditions of their particular time.

This distinction matters because a narrative can be historically decorated without being historically convincing.

### A. Build the world through its operations

Research the practical conditions governing what people can do, not just what their surroundings look like.

For example, English Heritage's reconstruction of the household of Joan de Valence at Goodrich Castle in 1296 draws on surviving financial accounts. Her household travelled between estates, bakers sometimes went ahead to prepare food, chaplains performed administrative work, and servants maintained horses and vehicles. These are details that create situations and determine what happens next.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.english-heritage.org.uk\&sz=32)

English Heritage



A useful scene might therefore begin with a problem involving transport, money, an absent official, or a disputed obligation. It does not need to begin by describing a medieval town.

Research the machinery that makes events possible. For each setting, establish the relevant political authority, social ranks, means of travel, methods of communication, food supply, occupations, legal customs, religious practices, and military obligations.

The date and region must be specific. Eleventh-century England and fifteenth-century Burgundy cannot share an interchangeable set of weapons, institutions, and customs. The Metropolitan Museum of Art's histories of medieval armour and knighthood demonstrate substantial changes in equipment and military society across the period.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.metmuseum.org\&sz=32)

The Metropolitan Museum of Art

+1



### B. Make historical thinking credible

A common mistake is to give medieval characters modern attitudes and then cover their dialogue with archaic vocabulary.

A more credible method is to establish what a character knows, what they believe is possible, what authorities they recognise, and what consequences they expect.

A knight might make decisions involving wages, honour, obligations to a lord, property, family interests, or prospects of ransom. None of those considerations should automatically make him honourable or dishonourable.

Likewise, peasants, merchants, servants, women of noble households, clergy, and soldiers should not be reduced to a few predetermined characteristics. Their circumstances restrict their choices, but do not dictate identical personalities.

### C. Preserve the discipline of nonfiction

There is one important distinction in your proposed approach.

If the people, conversations, and events are invented, the work is technically historically grounded fiction written in a nonfiction style. If you intend actual nonfiction, invented dialogue and unrecorded private thoughts cannot be presented as documented fact.

The Nieman Foundation's discussion of factual storytelling explicitly distinguishes narrative technique from fabrication. Writers can arrange documented material dramatically without creating unsupported events.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://niemanstoryboard.org\&sz=32)

Nieman Storyboard



For historical work, separate three kinds of material:

- Documented: Supported directly by primary sources or reliable historical scholarship.
- Inferred: Plausible conclusions from documented circumstances, identified as interpretations.
- Invented: Scenes, actions, dialogue, or individuals created for the narrative.

That distinction prevents vivid writing from producing false historical certainty.

### D. Write through cause and consequence

Each substantial scene should have a practical reason to exist. Someone is attempting something. Another person, an institution, a limitation, or an unexpected event interferes. What follows changes the available choices.

You do not have to end every scene with a dramatic revelation. Some situations remain unresolved. Some conversations accomplish little. Historical people, like contemporary people, acted on incomplete information and often misunderstood what was happening.

John McPhee's essay [Structure](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2013/01/14/structure) makes an important point about narrative nonfiction: factual material does not eliminate the writer's responsibility to organise it into a compelling progression. The arrangement creates much of the story's momentum.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.newyorker.com\&sz=32)

The New Yorker



My judgment: Documentary restraint is more valuable here than elaborate historical language. Use precise modern prose, historically credible decisions, and enough ordinary procedure to make events seem independent of the author's desire for drama.

## 2. Writing medieval battles realistically, at the scale of armies

A battle is not a thousand individual duels happening simultaneously. It is an encounter between organised groups trying to accomplish objectives under severe practical constraints.

That is the central principle for writing convincing battle narratives.

### A. Understand how medieval armies actually fought

Research from Swansea University's historians shows that, in Western Europe between 1000 and 1300, major pitched battles were less common than raids, skirmishes, and sieges. Commanders risked losing armies, territory, and political authority by committing to a decisive confrontation.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.swansea.ac.uk\&sz=32)

Swansea University



The historical literature on cohesion in medieval armies also challenges the idea that medieval warriors simply rushed forward in disorder. Formations, discipline, leadership, mutual support, and the ability to manoeuvre together were important.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://deremilitari.org\&sz=32)

deremilitari.org



That gives battle writing a different foundation.

Before describing combat, the writer needs to know what the commanders want, where their troops are positioned, what prevents them from moving freely, what information they possess, and what will happen if a section of the army fails.

### B. Build the battle as a sequence of military events

Deployment

Establish the ground, approaches, formations, reserves, vulnerable flanks, and routes of withdrawal. Avoid revealing everything through an ordinary soldier who could not possibly see the whole field.

Orders and movement

Show how commands reach formations and what delays, competing priorities, or misunderstandings interfere. A command from the centre does not instantly reorganise every part of an army.

Engagement

Establish which formations make contact, where pressure is concentrated, which troops remain uncommitted, and what happens when soldiers cannot maintain their positions.

Disruption and decisions

Let the outcome develop from events: a formation advances too far, support fails to arrive, troops misidentify a withdrawal, an opening appears, or a commander commits a reserve.

Collapse, retreat, and aftermath

Establish who remains organised, who can escape, who pursues, who is captured, and what the result changes. Do not end automatically when an important individual dies.

This is a framework, not a fixed formula. Some battles are decided by events before close contact. Others involve repeated advances, withdrawals, and renewed fighting.

### C. Logistics must change the battle

The research is particularly clear here.

In Feeding Mars: Military Purveyance in the Long Fourteenth Century, the historian's starting point is that tactical success depends on the practical arrangements supplying an army. Food, provisions, and methods of distribution are not secondary concerns.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.cambridge.org\&sz=32)

Fourteenth Century England



The scholarly study Besieging Bedford: Military Logistics in 1224 examines how a documented siege required a major reallocation of resources and military priorities.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.cambridge.org\&sz=32)

Journal of Medieval Military History



For writing purposes, supply should affect decisions rather than appear as a paragraph of historical background. A shortage can force movement. A damaged crossing can delay reinforcements. A commander might abandon an advantageous position because remaining there is no longer sustainable.

### D. Study a battle as a chain of decisions: Hattin, 1187

The [four medieval accounts of the Battle of Hattin](https://deremilitari.org/2014/01/the-battle-of-hattin-1187-four-accounts/) offer an especially useful case study.

The Crusader army's difficulty obtaining water, harassment during its march, decisions about where to camp, and the ability of its commanders to organise their forces all figure in the surviving accounts.

The battle is interesting before its final confrontation because the movement and condition of the army have already narrowed the commanders' choices. The four accounts also disagree on details and interpret responsibility differently.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://deremilitari.org\&sz=32)

deremilitari.org



That is a valuable model. A battle narrative becomes compelling when the reader understands how decisions limit later possibilities, not merely who was brave or who killed the most opponents.

### E. Control the scale of narration

A historian writing retrospectively can explain an entire battle using multiple accounts. A soldier within the battle cannot.

If the narration stays with an individual, the reader should receive information through what that person can observe, hear, recognise, or learn from others. The narrator should not suddenly provide casualty totals, identify a distant commander's intentions, or explain a manoeuvre that nobody nearby understands.

A wider historical narrator can move between opposing armies, but those movements should be deliberate and evidence-based.

My judgment: The essential skill is making battlefield developments intelligible without making them predictable. The writer should know precisely what is happening while allowing participants to act on imperfect knowledge. The tactical history provides the structure; the writing determines how much of it the reader learns at any moment.

## 3. Writing one-on-one and multiple-opponent combat while demonstrating skill

Individual combat requires a different kind of research. Here, the important questions are about distance, timing, control of weapons, available space, protection, and the training of the participants.

### A. Study historical fighting systems

Medieval fighting was not simply two people trading sword blows until one collapsed.

The surviving manuals show considerable technical sophistication.

[Broquel de acero](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/yJLgbRhsDClfYvzSiE7dFcr2ZyeXh6bSi6YxnUXkzfEtQQvNx5jDszQaYGIRRlWy5eaDIzdwd_2pgJ1fmjT5yiKzoT1v2q1GgMVIYEzMHA_IgrBhEYL8bLCX-1o6pqe8fm1fHCd32eXQhNrjUigkHnJuLGtsBmxIwIa0Qs5TGl8?purpose=inline)

Royal Armouries MS I.33

Early 14th century, German sword-and-buckler fencing

[The Olympics Go Medieval Brewminate: A Bold Blend of News and Ideas](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/pQQZyHb9n8r5M2OMFVh941_2ln4feB6GkH8SpPWdw5SyjOncu58L4JezIBX4q6qeD8sin4SJPRBE-itxLsLpNxHDrSy2vG-wjYMAcoKPN2mIjOsmNphRpxd5fQJDU6GCSuRUhSDhtWUFZtpc1z7pLEY378w9dBMc83sGU7PK33A?purpose=inline)

Fior di Battaglia

Circa 1410, wrestling, swords, daggers, polearms, armoured and mounted combat

Royal Armouries identifies I.33 as the oldest known surviving European fencing manual, while the Getty's copy of Fiore dei Liberi's Fior di Battaglia documents a broad range of martial techniques.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://shop.royalarmouries.org\&sz=32)

Royal Armouries Museum Shop

+1



These are valuable sources for describing how trained fighters approached combat. They are not proof that every soldier used the same techniques, or that the formal encounters shown in the manuscripts accurately reproduce chaotic battlefield fighting.

### B. Show skill through decisions, not effortless killing

A skilled fighter's superiority can appear in how they position themselves, recognise an opponent's intention, avoid committing too early, exploit an opening, or recover from a mistake.

Skill also differs by discipline. A capable swordsman is not automatically an expert wrestler, mounted fighter, or commander.

Consider the distinction:

A weak combat scene has the protagonist block three simultaneous attacks, spin, and kill three opponents.

A stronger one has the protagonist recognise that staying in the open permits several attackers to engage. He withdraws toward a restricted passage, forces the attackers to interfere with one another, and deals with one immediate threat. Another opponent moves to cut off his escape.

The second version demonstrates judgment. It still allows the opposition to make intelligent decisions and does not guarantee the protagonist's survival.

### C. Multiple opponents require spatial logic

The writer must know where every combatant is, even if the viewpoint character cannot account for them all.

If three people attack one defender, each attacker has a position, a weapon, a possible route of approach, and a limited amount of space in which to act. They may cooperate, obstruct one another, hesitate, or exploit a distraction.

Do not allow opponents to disappear from the action until their scheduled turn to attack.

For an outnumbered fighter, good decisions might include denying an opponent room to move, putting an obstacle between combatants, withdrawing before being surrounded, or accepting a less advantageous confrontation to avoid a worse one.

These are situational principles, not universal guarantees. Three organised attackers are a serious threat to an individual, however well trained.

### D. Armour changes the mechanics

Two historical misconceptions are especially important.

First, armour is not easily defeated by simply cutting through it. Mail and plate offer different forms of protection, and techniques and weapons need to suit the armour being encountered. The Metropolitan Museum of Art explains how armour reduces the effectiveness of many cutting attacks while leaving no wearer completely invulnerable.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.metmuseum.org\&sz=32)

The Metropolitan Museum of Art



Second, armour does not make someone helpless after falling. The Met documents the mobility of properly fitted field armour. An experimental study published in 2011 nevertheless found substantially higher energy expenditure for walking and running in armour.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.metmuseum.org\&sz=32)

The Metropolitan Museum of Art

+1



So neither the immobile knight nor the tireless armoured warrior is convincing.

### E. Select the movements that matter

Carla Hoch, a trained fighter and author writing for Writer's Digest, advises selecting pivotal actions rather than transcribing every movement of a confrontation.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.writersdigest.com\&sz=32)

Writers Digest



That approach fits your objective.

The reader usually needs to understand an attack, the response that changes the situation, and its consequence. A long inventory of parries, feints, cuts, and steps can obscure the fight.

A useful way to test a close-combat passage is to ask whether the reader can tell:

- Who has the immediate advantage.
- What action changes that advantage.
- Why the protagonist's response demonstrates training rather than luck.
- What prevents the same response from working indefinitely.

My judgment: Competence is most convincing when it has limits. Make the character demonstrably better at reading distance, timing, or positioning, but allow another trained opponent to recognise and counter those advantages. Fighting should involve adaptation, not a showcase of uninterrupted success.

## 4. How to write narrative that does not sound like AI

This is the most important stylistic question, because historical accuracy and technically correct combat will not rescue lifeless prose.

### What research actually shows

A November 2025 study in Humanities and Social Sciences Communications compared human-written short fiction with stories generated by GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Llama. The researchers found greater stylistic variation among human authors, while outputs from individual language models tended to cluster more closely together.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.nature.com\&sz=32)

Humanities and Social Sciences Communications



Another 2025 study examined 2,200 college admissions essays across three preregistered experiments. The researchers found that additional human-written essays contributed more novel ideas to the overall collection than additional GPT-4 essays.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://doi.org\&sz=32)

ScienceDirect



These studies support your concern about stylistic uniformity. They do not prove that repetition, motifs, polished language, or predictable structure are uniquely AI characteristics. Human writers also produce formulaic work, and these particular results cannot establish how every current model writes.

The practical problem is broader than repetition. It is prose that feels assembled from familiar literary conventions rather than selected for the particular situation being described.

### How to correct it

Stop explaining significance. A narrator should not repeatedly tell the reader that a decision was important, a silence was meaningful, or an encounter changed everything. Establish the action and its consequences. Trust the reader to infer what is warranted.

Avoid automatic symbolic patterns. A recurring object, gesture, colour, or phrase should not be introduced merely to represent a character's internal conflict. Repetition can be useful when it conveys routine or factual continuity, but it should not become a substitute for developing events.

Remove stock emotional descriptions. References to tightening throats, racing hearts, held breaths, and the weight of memory become interchangeable when they serve only to announce emotion. Describe what a person does, says, refuses, misunderstands, or leaves unfinished.

Allow ordinary behaviour. Not every conversation requires a revelation. Someone may continue an administrative task during an argument. Another person can respond to a serious accusation by asking about something practical. Characters do not always explain themselves, reach understanding, or act consistently with their stated beliefs.

Keep procedural passages. Describe tasks accurately, with appropriate vocabulary and enough physical sequence to make them comprehensible. Not every piece of work needs accompanying introspection. A historical world becomes more convincing when people carry out ordinary responsibilities without the narration insisting on their meaning.

Vary the prose according to its purpose. A description of military deployment may need careful, extended sentences. A disagreement about payment can be almost entirely dialogue. A journey might pass in a short paragraph. An administrative exchange may take longer on the page than a violent encounter. Do not force every passage into the same rhythm.

Do not manufacture elegant endings. Scenes can stop when the narrative's attention needs to move elsewhere. An unresolved matter may remain unresolved without the narrator acknowledging it. Avoid ending every scene with an observation that echoes its opening or converts the events into a lesson.

John McPhee's essay [Omission](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2015/09/14/omission) is useful here. His central craft principle is selection: the writer must decide which material to retain and which to leave the reader to infer.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.newyorker.com\&sz=32)

The New Yorker



### What this means at sentence level

GENERIC, OVERWRITTEN VERSION

The battlefield became a storm of iron and blood, as each soldier struggled beneath the terrible weight of fear and duty.

GROUNDED VERSION, HYPOTHETICAL

The order to halt reached the front rank too late. The men behind continued forward, driving the right-hand file against the hedge. Their captain could no longer reach them.

The second passage does not depend on figurative language or an asserted emotional atmosphere. The reader can reconstruct an actual problem. It also provides an opportunity for a character to make a decision, with a result that need not be successful.

George Orwell's [Politics and the English Language](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/) offers an older, useful explanation of why prefabricated phrases weaken prose: they allow a writer to substitute familiar wording for precise thought.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.orwellfoundation.com\&sz=32)

The Orwell Foundation



### How to construct the narrative itself

Before writing a chapter, decide whose experience or evidence controls the account, what situation is already underway, what information is missing, and what developments will change the available choices.

Then decide where to use scenes and where to summarise.

A complete narrative does not have to dramatise every event. Weeks of travel can be compressed. A minor conversation can receive several pages if it changes an agreement. The time devoted to an event should reflect its narrative function, not merely its apparent importance.

Use the same discipline for transitions. Do not invent a dramatic bridge every time the location changes. A direct change of date, place, or activity is often sufficient.

Finally, revise in separate passes. John McPhee's [Draft No. 4](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2013/04/29/draft-no-4) describes repeated revision as essential to finding exact language, rather than expecting a finished style from the first draft.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.newyorker.com\&sz=32)

The New Yorker



For this project, I would use four editing passes:

1. Historical pass: Verify the period, institutions, equipment, behaviour, and limits of the evidence.
2. Causality pass: Check that events follow from established circumstances rather than occurring for dramatic convenience.
3. Combat pass: Trace positions, formations, available weapons, timing, and the information each participant possesses.
4. Language pass: Remove redundant explanations, imposed symbolism, recycled descriptions, artificial emotional conclusions, and phrases chosen primarily because they sound literary.

Do not try to make writing seem human by inserting mistakes or arbitrary irregularities. The objective is controlled prose with specific observations and a recognisable point of view.

# Research: How to write natural, realistic conversations in narrative

The central problem with artificial-sounding dialogue is that characters often speak as though they understand the author's intentions. They deliver information at convenient moments, respond too precisely, articulate emotions they would ordinarily conceal, and make every exchange seem more important than it is.

Natural conversation starts with how particular people behave toward one another, not with what the author wants them to say.

This applies to medieval narratives, modern fiction, military stories, domestic scenes, ordinary exchanges, arguments, and conversations involving several people.

## 1. What research reveals about real conversations

Three particularly useful sources are:

- [Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson's research on conversational turn-taking](https://www.conversationanalysis.org/schegloff-media-archive/simplest-systematics-for-turn-taking-language-1974/) (1974). Conversation is coordinated turn by turn, with participants continually responding to one another rather than following a predetermined sequence.&#x20;

  [image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.conversationanalysis.org\&sz=32)

  ISCA

- [Schegloff, Jefferson and Sacks on conversational repair](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language/article/preference-for-selfcorrection-in-the-organization-of-repair-in-conversation/5549B861FDE7180B75FA5C382821875E) (1977). Speakers routinely correct themselves, clarify what others mean, and deal with failures of understanding.&#x20;

  [image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.cambridge.org\&sz=32)

  Cambridge Core

- [George Saunders on writing realistic dialogue](https://www.writersdigest.com/be-inspired/wd-interview-george-saunders-structure-outlining-lincoln-in-the-bardo), Writer's Digest. Saunders describes recording a conversation between his mother and grandmother. When transcribed, it was almost incomprehensible without the speakers' shared context. His lesson was that convincing literary dialogue must recreate the experience of listening to conversation without copying every verbal irregularity.&#x20;

  [image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.writersdigest.com\&sz=32)

  Writer's Digest



The implication is important: Realistic dialogue is not necessarily verbatim speech. It is selectively written speech that preserves the behaviour of actual conversation.

### Seven behaviours worth understanding

1. People don't always answer the question asked. They might answer the implied question, address something said earlier, misunderstand, or attend to another concern.
2. People assume shared knowledge. Friends, relatives, fellow soldiers, and colleagues frequently leave names, explanations, and circumstances unstated.
3. People repair misunderstandings. They correct a word, question what someone meant, or discover that they have been discussing different things.
4. People change subjects. Sometimes there is an obvious association. Sometimes the other person simply has something else to deal with.
5. People speak according to their relationships. Familiarity, authority, resentment, affection, embarrassment, and social expectations affect what can be said.
6. People leave things unsaid without intending to be mysterious. They may see no reason to explain, lack the vocabulary, or assume their meaning is already understood.
7. People speak for ordinary reasons. They request things, report small problems, make arrangements, complain, joke, ask for confirmation, or talk while working.

Do not put all seven into every conversation. Doing so would create another obvious writing formula.

## 2. Think about the people before thinking about the dialogue

For every conversation, establish five facts about the participants.

| Question                                     | What it changes                                                                                                |
| -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| What are they doing?                         | Whether speaking is their main activity or something happening alongside work, travel, eating, or another task |
| How do they know each other?                 | Familiarity, formality, shared history, acceptable jokes, and what needs explaining                            |
| What does each person know?                  | Assumptions, omissions, surprises, and misunderstandings                                                       |
| What does each person presently care about?  | Which remarks they notice, ignore, challenge, or pursue                                                        |
| What are they willing to say to this person? | Directness, politeness, caution, defensiveness, and authority                                                  |

One character may want to discuss a missing horse. Another may be occupied with recovering unpaid wages. Neither knows they are supposed to participate in a scene about the broader military campaign.

That difference creates a conversation that follows the characters' circumstances instead of the author's outline.

Most importantly, a character's reason for speaking can be extremely simple. They may want someone to move aside, pass a tool, stop talking, or answer a question. Not every line needs a concealed emotional objective or a connection to the novel's central conflict.

That is where I would depart from some conventional dialogue advice. Insisting that every sentence advance the plot can produce exactly the relentless dramatic efficiency you're trying to avoid.

## 3. Let the conversation follow its own logic

A useful distinction comes from the Writing Excuses episode [Writing Conversational Dialogue](https://writingexcuses.com/18-28-writing-conversational-dialogue/), featuring Mary Robinette Kowal and other professional writers.

Every line has two separate explanations: why the author included it and why the character actually said it. If the second explanation is weak, the dialogue will probably feel manufactured.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://writingexcuses.com\&sz=32)

Writing Excuses



Imagine two people preparing a wagon for a journey. The author knows they will eventually discover that someone has stolen supplies.

The characters do not know this.

One is trying to finish loading. The other is checking an inventory. Their conversation should initially reflect those concerns, not jump directly into a dramatic discussion of betrayal.

### Example: Artificial dialogue

> "Our supplies are dangerously low," Martin said. "If we cannot find the missing grain, the men will go hungry."
>
> "Then we must discover who took it. I fear someone within our own company has betrayed us."
>
> "You may be right. But we cannot allow suspicion to divide us."

Everything is arranged for the reader. The characters state the problem, interpret it, and discuss its significance. They even agree about the appropriate moral response.

### Example: A more natural exchange

The clerk cut the twine and opened the sack. He poured a measure of grain into the bin.

"That one's for the west cart," Martin said.

"It still can be."

"Not if you empty it."

"It's damp underneath."

"All of it?"

"Don't know yet."

Martin checked the tally.

"Where's the other sack?"

"Which?"

"From the mill. There should be six."

"There were six."

"There's five."

"Ask Hal."

"Hal's gone."

"Where?"

"Home, he said."

The clerk tipped the sack. A clump remained against the seam. He scraped it loose with the wooden measure.

"He was supposed to stay till tomorrow," Martin said.

"That's what I heard."

"Did you pay him?"

"No."

"Then why's he gone?"

"Ask him."

Someone outside called for the cart key. Martin went to the doorway.

This exchange is not an exact transcription of real speech. It is an invented example illustrating several features of naturalistic conversation.

The speakers do not explain information they already share. They are occupied with a task. They repeat words because they are trying to establish a practical fact, not because the narration requires emphasis. Neither articulates the situation's larger implications. The dialogue does not resolve the missing supplies.

Notice also that the physical action is not ornamental. The clerk has an actual job to complete. He continues doing it while talking.

This is one way to achieve realism without using constant hesitations, exaggerated accents, or dramatic interruptions.

## 4. Ordinary conversations should not all be confrontations

A particularly useful academic source is Ludmila Urbanova's [Phatic Communion and Small Talk in Fictional Dialogues](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/slcs.85.19urb) (2007), which examines everyday conversational features in fiction.

Small talk can establish social contact and reproduce familiar patterns of interaction. Its function is not limited to delivering information or creating conflict.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.benjamins.com\&sz=32)

John Benjamins Publishing Catalog



This matters because ordinary conversations take several different forms.

Practical exchanges: Someone asks for a tool, reports a problem, corrects a measurement, or arranges a task. The conversation may finish with the task completed.

Familiar exchanges: Relatives or long-standing companions refer to people and events without explaining them. One may ignore a question because they already know why it was asked.

Unequal exchanges: A servant addressing an employer, a soldier reporting to a commander, or a witness answering a magistrate may speak more carefully because the other person has authority.

Casual exchanges: People gossip, joke, complain, repeat a familiar story, or discuss something with no immediate bearing on the central plot.

Serious exchanges: A disagreement, accusation, negotiation, confession, or warning may require longer and more deliberate speech.

The mistake is giving all five the same dramatic rhythm.

A routine conversation does not need to evolve into an argument. Nor should every character become unusually articulate whenever the subject matters.

## 5. Let characters talk differently to different people

Distinct dialogue is not simply a matter of giving each character a recognisable vocabulary.

The same man may speak bluntly to his brother, cautiously to his lord, affectionately to his child, and formally before a court.

What changes is not necessarily his fundamental personality. It is what he believes he can say, what he expects the other person to understand, and what consequences the exchange might carry.

Mary Robinette Kowal discusses pacing, sentence structure, and attitude as distinguishable dimensions of written character voice.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://writingexcuses.com\&sz=32)

Writing Excuses



A character may also contradict themselves naturally. Someone who refuses to explain a decision to a subordinate may spend twenty minutes justifying the same decision to a friend. Someone who talks constantly among friends may say almost nothing in unfamiliar company.

Don't turn this into a rigid character-voice chart in which one person always uses fragments and another always speaks in formal sentences. Circumstances change how people talk.

## 6. Understand indirect answers without forcing subtext

The philosophical study of conversational implicature explains how an apparently indirect reply can nevertheless communicate a meaningful answer.

For example:

"Is he coming?"

"His horse is still here."

The second person has not directly answered the question, but the context may make the implication clear.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://plato.stanford.edu\&sz=32)

Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy



This is different from manufacturing subtext in every line.

Sometimes "His horse is still here" simply means that his horse is still there. Sometimes a character answers "No" without hesitation. Sometimes the two speakers misunderstand one another.

Avoid writing as though every practical remark secretly represents grief, jealousy, ambition, or betrayal. That gives ordinary dialogue an artificial literary weight.

## 7. How dialogue should interact with the surrounding narrative

Conversation is part of the narrative, not a separate script inserted into it.

The prose needs to manage the passage of time, select the relevant actions, maintain the viewpoint, and occasionally explain something the dialogue cannot establish naturally.

The author can summarise unimportant conversations. For example:

They spent the rest of the morning disputing the number of wagons and settled nothing.

There is no need to reproduce every argument if the specific wording does not matter.

Conversely, a seemingly insignificant exchange can deserve a full scene if its exact wording affects a later misunderstanding.

Do not attach a gesture to every line. Do not continually explain facial expressions or emotional reactions. A character can speak three times without requiring three descriptive interruptions.

And do not make every conversational passage end with a profound observation. Someone can leave because their work is finished, another person has arrived, or the exchange has become pointless.

## 8. How to stop the specific habits of AI-like dialogue

The most useful editing method is to look for sentences that exist mainly to help the author communicate with the reader.

Common symptoms include characters who explain shared history aloud, repeat the previous speaker's point before answering, understand complicated emotions immediately, or end conversations with unusually polished conclusions.

Other problems include dialogue that is permanently witty, uniformly confrontational, relentlessly brief, or filled with artificial pauses and significant silences.

But removing every repetition and interruption is not the answer. Actual conversation contains repair, clarification, and recurring phrases. The original research by Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson, including their [1974 turn-taking study](https://www.conversationanalysis.org/schegloff-media-archive/simplest-systematics-for-turn-taking-language-1974/) and [1977 repair study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language/article/preference-for-selfcorrection-in-the-organization-of-repair-in-conversation/5549B861FDE7180B75FA5C382821875E), documents these mechanisms.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.conversationanalysis.org\&sz=32)

The question is whether the repetition belongs to the speaker's immediate behaviour or has been inserted to reinforce the story.

A practical revision technique comes from Writing Excuses: temporarily remove every third line of dialogue and see whether actions, context, and the remaining responses can carry some of the omitted meaning. This is an exercise, not a rule for finished prose.&#x20;

I would also read conversations aloud, separately from the narrative. If several characters sound like the same articulate person conducting a series of orderly debates, the dialogue needs further work.

## 9. Applying this to medieval dialogue

For medieval narratives, there is an additional historical limitation: we cannot directly recover most ordinary spoken exchanges.

A Cambridge University Press chapter on historical pragmatics identifies medieval sources such as court proceedings, depositions, sermons, drama, and letters, but distinguishes among records based on speech, writing intended to be spoken, and writing that merely resembles speech.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.cambridge.org\&sz=32)

Pragmatics in the History of English



Jeremy Goldberg's research on later medieval court records also explains how clerks' recording practices and translations shaped the words preserved from witnesses.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.cambridge.org\&sz=32)

Women, Agency and the Law, 1300–1700



So historically convincing medieval dialogue is not achieved by throwing in thee, thou, and forsooth.

Instead, research the period's forms of address, social obligations, religious assumptions, occupations, and the consequences of speaking out of turn.

Then write comprehensible dialogue in which people act according to those circumstances.

An ordinary medieval labourer discussing unpaid wages should sound like someone trying to obtain payment, not like an actor performing a historical identity. A trained commander may use precise military terminology, but that does not require him to give an eloquent speech whenever he issues an order.

The surrounding historical situation does most of the work.

## A method to use when drafting

Before writing a conversation, briefly imagine the exchange without the reader present.

What would these people discuss if nobody were listening? What happened just before the conversation? Does one person know something the other doesn't? Is one trying to finish another task? How familiar are they? Is there any reason for them to be especially careful, polite, or direct?

Write their first exchange based on those circumstances. For each subsequent reply, consider what the listener would actually attend to, rather than automatically producing the most useful response for the plot.

Afterward, edit for clarity. Preserve the behaviour but compress unnecessary detours.
